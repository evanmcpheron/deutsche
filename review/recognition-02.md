These words often change **tone, expectation, or speaker attitude** more than the basic facts. Do not hunt for one English translation. Read the situation, compare the sentence without the particle, and decide what the speaker is doing.

---

## Part A — Read the Situation and Tone

**1.** A friend is looking at the wrong page. You casually ask them to look at your page for a moment. Which sentence best fits?

   a. Schau denn hier. \
   b. Schau mal hier. \
   c. Schau ja hier. \
   d. Schau eben hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *mal* makes a short imperative smaller and more casual: *Schau mal hier.* The basic request stays the same; the tone becomes less sharp.

</details>

**2.** Your friend says, *Ich bin müde.* You want a warm follow-up about what led to the tiredness, not a change of topic. Which question fits best?

   a. Was hast du mal gemacht? \
   b. Was hast du eigentlich gemacht? \
   c. Was hast du ja gemacht? \
   d. Was hast du denn gemacht?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** In a follow-up question, *denn* signals genuine interest and makes the question conversational rather than clipped.

</details>

**3.** Someone says, *Du kommst heute nicht mit.* You are coming and want to contradict the negative statement in one word. What do you say?

   a. Doch! \
   b. Ja! \
   c. Eben! \
   d. Mal!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Standalone *doch* contradicts a negative statement or negative question: “Yes, I am.” Plain *ja* does not perform that clear contradiction.

</details>

**4.** You have been talking about travel. A new thought occurs to you, and you casually ask your friend where they live. Which sentence best signals that small change of topic?

   a. Wo wohnst du ja? \
   b. Wo wohnst du mal? \
   c. Wo wohnst du eigentlich? \
   d. Wo wohnst du eben?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In questions, *eigentlich* often means something like “by the way / come to think of it.” It introduces a slightly new or off-topic question naturally.

</details>

**5.** You enter a shop and see that a simple bottle of water costs six euros. You react with surprise. Which sentence best fits?

   a. Das ist mal teuer! \
   b. Das ist ja teuer! \
   c. Das ist denn teuer! \
   d. Das ist halt teuer!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Mid-sentence particle *ja* can mark a shared observation or surprise: *Das ist ja teuer!* It is not the ordinary answer word “yes” here.

</details>

**6.** The bus is cancelled. There is no useful alternative except walking, and you accept that without arguing. Which sentence best matches that attitude?

   a. Dann gehen wir denn zu Fuß. \
   b. Dann gehen wir ja zu Fuß. \
   c. Dann gehen wir mal zu Fuß. \
   d. Dann gehen wir eben zu Fuß.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Particle *eben* presents the choice as the practical, resigned result: “Then we’ll just walk.” It treats the situation as something to accept.

</details>

**7.** A friend in Munich says the café is expensive, but that is simply how cafés there are. Which sentence has the casual spoken “that’s just how it is” feeling taught on the *halt* page?

   a. Das ist halt so. \
   b. Das ist denn so. \
   c. Das ist mal so. \
   d. Das ist eigentlich so.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Particle *halt* is a casual, often southern-flavoured way to express resigned acceptance. At early A1 it is mainly for recognition, not production.

</details>

**8.** Your friend hesitates about joining the group. You warmly encourage them to come. Which sentence best fits?

   a. Komm ja mit! \
   b. Komm eben mit! \
   c. Komm doch mit! \
   d. Komm denn mit!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *doch* pushes gently against hesitation: *Komm doch mit!* is warmer and more persuasive than the plain *Komm mit!*

</details>

## Part B — What Changes With the Particle?

**9.** Compare **Warte!** and **Warte mal!** What does *mal* mainly change?

   a. It changes a statement into a question. \
   b. It makes the instruction smaller and more casual. \
   c. It means the waiting happened once before. \
   d. It makes the instruction more formal.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** As a modal particle with an imperative, *mal* softens the social force. It does not materially change what action is requested.

</details>

**10.** Compare **Wo ist der Bahnhof?** and **Wo ist denn der Bahnhof?** What does *denn* mainly add?

   a. A reason: “because.” \
   b. A correction of the listener. \
   c. Interest and a more conversational tone. \
   d. A command to go to the station.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Particle *denn* belongs in questions and often makes them sound engaged or curious. Its conjunction meaning “because” is a separate use.

</details>

**11.** Compare **Das weißt du.** and **Das weißt du doch.** What does *doch* add most naturally?

   a. A reminder that the speaker expects this to be shared knowledge. \
   b. A neutral statement with no added stance. \
   c. A question about whether the listener knows it. \
   d. Resignation that nothing can change.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Here *doch* pushes against an implied contrary idea: “But you know that / you do know that.” The factual claim stays the same, but the speaker reminds or insists.

</details>

**12.** Compare **Wo arbeitest du?** and **Wo arbeitest du eigentlich?** What does *eigentlich* most naturally suggest in the second sentence?

   a. The speaker is angry about the job. \
   b. The speaker knows the answer already. \
   c. The question is a command. \
   d. The question has just occurred to the speaker or shifts the topic slightly.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** In a question, *eigentlich* often marks a friendly aside or new thought rather than a hard corrective “actually.”

</details>

**13.** Compare **Es ist nicht weit.** and **Es ist ja nicht weit.** In the second sentence, what is the speaker most likely doing?

   a. Asking whether it is far. \
   b. Appealing to something obvious or already known, often reassuringly. \
   c. Saying the distance has changed. \
   d. Accepting an unpleasant fact with resignation.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Particle *ja* can present information as shared or obvious: “It isn’t far, after all.” The distance itself has not changed.

</details>

**14.** Compare **Das ist so.** and **Das ist eben so.** What does *eben* add?

   a. Surprise. \
   b. A request. \
   c. Acceptance that the fact is simply the way things are. \
   d. A contradiction of a negative statement.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *eben* turns a plain statement into a resigned “that’s just how it is.” It often closes down further argument rather than adding new factual information.

</details>

**15.** Compare **Es ist teuer.** and **Es ist halt teuer.** Which description is most accurate?

   a. *halt* turns the sentence into formal written German. \
   b. *halt* means the price was expensive earlier. \
   c. *halt* expresses surprise at discovering the price. \
   d. *halt* adds casual resigned acceptance of the price.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The *halt* sentence treats the expense as an unchangeable fact: “It’s just expensive.” The lesson marks this use as casual and mainly passive for early A1.

</details>

**16.** Which pair keeps essentially the same requested action but changes a direct imperative into a more casual request?

   a. **Komm her. / Komm mal her.** \
   b. **Komm mit. / Komm doch nicht mit.** \
   c. **Wo bist du? / Wo bist du denn nicht?** \
   d. **Das ist teuer. / Das ist eben billig.**

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Komm mal her* requests the same action as *Komm her* but makes it feel routine and less pointed. The other pairs change factual content or are unnatural.

</details>

## Part C — Dialogue, Interpretation, and Near Neighbours

**17.** Complete the dialogue naturally.

A: Ich kann heute nicht kommen.\
B: Warum ___ nicht?

   a. mal \
   b. ja \
   c. denn \
   d. halt

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Warum denn nicht?* is a common follow-up chunk. The *denn* marks interest in the reason after something was just said.

</details>

**18.** Someone asks **Hast du keine Zeit?** You do have time. Which answer is correct?

   a. Ja. \
   b. Doch. \
   c. Eben. \
   d. Eigentlich.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The question is negative, and you are contradicting that negative assumption. German uses *doch*.

</details>

**19.** You ask a friend, “How old are you, by the way?” Which sentence best matches the source lesson?

   a. Wie alt bist du eigentlich? \
   b. Wie alt bist du halt? \
   c. Wie alt bist du ja? \
   d. Wie alt bist du mal?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *eigentlich* is natural when a question arises as an aside or slight topic shift: *Wie alt bist du eigentlich?*

</details>

**20.** Your friend says the restaurant is closed. You strongly agree: “Exactly!” Which one-word response is taught as safe and useful?

   a. Denn! \
   b. Mal! \
   c. Ja! \
   d. Eben!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Standalone **Eben!** means “Exactly!” and is the one productive exception specifically encouraged on the *eben* page.

</details>

**21.** Which sentence uses *ja* as a **particle**, not as the ordinary word “yes”?

   a. Ja, ich komme mit. \
   b. Das ist ja interessant. \
   c. Ja, gern. \
   d. Ja, danke.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** In *Das ist ja interessant*, *ja* sits unstressed inside a statement and adds a shared-observation or surprise effect. The other examples are ordinary “yes.”

</details>

**22.** Which sentence uses *eben* to mean **“just now”**, not as the resignation particle?

   a. Er war eben hier. \
   b. Das ist eben so. \
   c. Dann gehen wir eben. \
   d. So ist das eben.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Er war eben hier* means he was here a moment ago. In the other sentences, *eben* is the modal particle of resigned acceptance.

</details>

**23.** Which sentence uses *halt* as a **particle**, not as the shouted command “Stop!” or the noun meaning a stop?

   a. Halt! Nicht weitergehen! \
   b. Nächster Halt: Hauptbahnhof. \
   c. Wir fahren halt später. \
   d. Der Halt ist kurz.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In *Wir fahren halt später*, *halt* is the conversational resignation particle. The other examples use the separate “stop” meanings.

</details>

**24.** Which sentence is the most natural friendly request to a person you know well?

   a. Du hilfst denn mir. \
   b. Hilf ja mir. \
   c. Hilf eben mir. \
   d. Hilf mir mal.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Hilf mir mal* uses the source-taught pattern of *mal* with a short request. The other particles do not fit this function here.

</details>

**25.** A friend says, *Ich habe eigentlich keine Zeit.* Which interpretation best matches the lesson?

   a. The speaker has unlimited time. \
   b. The statement is less final than plain *Ich habe keine Zeit* and may leave room for an exception. \
   c. The speaker is asking what time it is. \
   d. The speaker is contradicting someone who said they had no time.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** In statements, *eigentlich* can create an implied contrast: “I don’t really have time …” often with an unspoken or following “but.”

</details>

**26.** Which sentence best shows *doch* as a reminder of shared knowledge rather than as standalone contradiction?

   a. Doch! \
   b. Doch du kommst morgen. \
   c. Hast du doch Zeit? \
   d. Du kommst doch morgen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Du kommst doch morgen* appeals to what the speaker believes is already established: “You are coming tomorrow, right / remember.” Standalone *Doch!* is the separate contradiction use.

</details>

**27.** A server says something quickly. You want a polite, casual “Have a look here” with **Sie**. Which source-taught pattern fits?

   a. Schauen Sie mal hier. \
   b. Schauen Sie denn hier. \
   c. Schauen Sie halt hier. \
   d. Schauen Sie ja hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *mal* works with formal *Sie* too: *Schauen Sie mal hier.* It softens the request without changing the formal pronoun.

</details>

**28.** Which statement about *eben* and *halt* matches the source material?

   a. *halt* is formal; *eben* is only slang. \
   b. They have completely unrelated particle meanings. \
   c. As particles they are near-synonyms for resigned acceptance; *halt* is more casual and more southern-flavoured. \
   d. Both should be actively inserted into beginner speech whenever possible.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The lessons present them as near-synonyms in this particle use. *eben* is more neutral; *halt* is more casual and regionally southern in flavour. Both are mainly for recognition at this level.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Explain

For these final questions, answer before opening the solution. Keep production limited to the chunks the lessons mark as safe.

**29. Interpretation.** In one short phrase, say what *denn* adds to **Was machst du denn?** compared with **Was machst du?**

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** It adds **interest and a more conversational tone**.

**Explanation:** The factual question is the same. *denn* mainly changes how the question lands socially.

</details>

**30. Changed sentence.** Start with **Komm mit!** Your friend is hesitant, and you want to encourage them warmly without changing the requested action. Add the particle taught for that purpose.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Komm doch mit!**

**Explanation:** *doch* pushes gently against the hesitation and makes the invitation more persuasive.

</details>

**31. Controlled retrieval.** Someone says **Du magst keinen Kaffee.** You do like coffee. Give the one-word contradiction.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Doch!**

**Explanation:** This is the active, rule-based use of *doch*: contradict a negative statement or question.

</details>

**32. Classification.** In **Er war eben hier**, is *eben* the resignation particle or the ordinary time adverb?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** The **ordinary time adverb**: “just now / a moment ago.”

**Explanation:** The resignation particle appears in sentences such as *Das ist eben so.* Context and sentence function tell the two uses apart.

</details>

---

### What Success Looks Like Here

A strong result means you can hear the speaker's **stance** without forcing a literal English translation. You should especially recognize *mal* with casual requests, *denn* in interested questions, *doch* as pushback or contradiction, and *eigentlich* as a conversational aside. *ja*, *eben*, and *halt* can remain mostly passive.

Do not respond to a good score by forcing particles into every sentence. Recognition is the main goal of this review.
