Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

## Part A — Wo? or Wohin?

1. The lights have gone down and the adverts have started. You put your phone away after one last message.\
   *Ich bin ___ Kino.*\
   a. ins\
   b. in der\
   c. im\
   d. in die

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** You're sitting there already, so the sentence answers *Wo?* — dative, and *das Kino* is neuter, so *in dem* → *im*. Option a is the same building with the opposite meaning: *ins Kino* has you still walking towards it.

</details>

2. It starts raining hard while you're standing outside with a friend, so you both head for the door of the nearest café.\
   *Wir gehen ___ Café.*\
   a. ins\
   b. im\
   c. in die\
   d. nach

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Something is going inside → accusative, and *das Café* is neuter, so *in das* → *ins*. Option d is the reflex to watch: bare *nach* works for cities and most countries, never for an ordinary noun like this one.

</details>

3. A friend has started a new job. She spends her day behind a reception desk, and there are eighty rooms upstairs.\
   *Sie arbeitet ___ Hotel.*\
   a. ins\
   b. in einem\
   c. in ein\
   d. in einer

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Working somewhere is a position, not a journey → dative. *das Hotel* is neuter, and the dative of *ein* is *einem*. Option c is the accusative, which would only make sense if she were walking in through the door.

</details>

4. It's Saturday morning, you need new shoes, and the tram leaves in five minutes.\
   *Ich fahre ___ Stadt.*\
   a. in der\
   b. im\
   c. ins\
   d. in die

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A trip into town answers *Wohin?* → accusative, and *die Stadt* is feminine, so the article stays *die*. Option a is the trap worth learning by heart: *in der Stadt* is real German for driving about once you're already there.

</details>

5. The child has brushed his teeth and is walking down the hall towards his room.\
   *Das Kind geht ___ Bett.*\
   a. im\
   b. in die\
   c. in der\
   d. ins

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** He's on his way, so accusative: *in das* → *ins*. Option a describes the situation ten minutes later, when he's lying there. Options b and c bring a feminine article to a neuter noun.

</details>

6. A colleague phones you from her hotel in Zurich and you ask how the trip is going.\
   *Ich bin ___ Schweiz.*\
   a. in die\
   b. nach der\
   c. in der\
   d. in

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *die Schweiz* is one of the few countries that keeps its article, so *in* rather than *nach* — and she's there, so dative *der*. Option b tries to combine *nach* with an article, which German never does.

</details>

7. Your partner asks whether to put the pasta on. You're on the train and you'll be through the door an hour from now.\
   *Ich bin ___ zu Hause.*\
   a. in eine Stunde\
   b. in einer Stunde\
   c. nach einer Stunde\
   d. um eine Stunde

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Time with *in* is always dative — nothing is moving, so the accusative in option a has nothing to do. Option c is real German, but *nach* needs an event to come after; on its own it doesn't answer *when*.

</details>

8. The milk has run out, the shop shuts at eight, and you're pulling your jacket on.\
   *Ich gehe schnell ___ Supermarkt.*\
   a. im\
   b. ins\
   c. in der\
   d. in den

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *der Supermarkt* is masculine, and you're heading there → accusative *den*. Option a is grammatical and common — it just says you're already inside, which is not what the jacket suggests.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

9. **Ich fahre in der Stadt.** What does the sentence actually say?\
   a. Ich bin schon in der Stadt.\
   b. Ich fahre in die Stadt.\
   c. Ich komme aus der Stadt.\
   d. The sentence is incorrect.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Correct German, different idea: the dative makes it a location, so the driving happens inside town. The trip there needs the accusative. English speakers reach for the dative because *mit, zu* and *bei* trained them to.

</details>

10. The child was put to bed twenty minutes ago and is asleep. Which sentence fits?\
   a. Das Kind geht ins Bett.\
   b. Das Kind kommt ins Bett.\
   c. Das Kind ist im Bett.\
   d. Das Kind geht in die Küche.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Nothing is happening any more, so dative: *im Bett*. Options a and b are both still in motion, and d is a different room altogether. The single syllable *im* versus *ins* carries the whole difference.

</details>

11. A friend writes **Wir gehen ins Restaurant.** Which question does that answer?\
   a. Wo?\
   b. Wohin?\
   c. Woher?\
   d. Wann?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Accusative after a two-way preposition always answers *Wohin?*. Change *ins* to *im* and the very same sentence would answer *Wo?* instead.

</details>

12. Which sentence does **not** describe where somebody or something already is?\
   a. Ich bin im Zug.\
   b. Wir wohnen in der Nähe.\
   c. Ich stelle das Buch ins Regal.\
   d. Er ist in der Küche.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *ins* is *in das* — accusative, so the book is being moved. The other three are dative and report positions. Reading the article is faster than reading the verb once you're used to it.

</details>

13. A colleague sends you a message. Which one tells you she has **not** arrived yet?\
   a. Ich komme gleich ins Büro.\
   b. Ich bin im Büro.\
   c. Ich arbeite im Büro.\
   d. Ich sitze im Büro.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Only option a is accusative, and *gleich* confirms it — she's on her way. The other three are dative and place her at the desk. This *im* / *ins* pair is worth training your ear on specifically.

</details>

14. You've booked a flight to Istanbul for October.\
   *Im Herbst fliegen wir ___.*\
   a. in Istanbul\
   b. in die Istanbul\
   c. nach der Türkei\
   d. nach Istanbul

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A named city takes bare *nach*, with no article anywhere. Option a is where you'd be once you landed. Option c shows why the country and the city behave differently: *die Türkei* brings an article, and an article forces *in die Türkei*.

</details>

15. **Select all that apply.** Which sentences describe going **into** somewhere?\
   a. Wir fahren in die Schweiz.\
   b. Ich bin im Hotel.\
   c. Das Kind geht ins Bett.\
   d. Sie arbeitet in einem Büro.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c**

**Explanation:** Both are accusative — *die Schweiz* and *ins* — so both are destinations. Options b and d are dative and report where somebody is. The nouns give you nothing here; only the articles do.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

16. Complete the dialogue. B is on her way to work.

```text
A: Wo bist du?
B: Ich bin ___ Zug.
```

   a. ins\
   b. in den\
   c. in der\
   d. im

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A's question word settles the case before you read anything else: *wo* forces the dative, and *der Zug* is masculine → *in dem* → *im*. Option b is the accusative and would answer a question A didn't ask.

</details>

17. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Kommst du mit ___ Stadt?
B: Ja, gern. Ich brauche neue Schuhe.
```

   a. in der\
   b. in die\
   c. im\
   d. ins

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** B's reply is the clue: buying shoes means a trip is being planned, not a place being described → accusative *in die Stadt*. Option a would turn A's invitation into a statement about where they both already are.

</details>

18. Complete the dialogue. B is looking at the timetable on the pole.

```text
A: Wann kommt der Bus?
B: ___ fünf Minuten.
```

   a. In\
   b. Im\
   c. Nach\
   d. Um

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** A stretch of time before something happens takes *in* plus the dative: *in fünf Minuten*. Option d is for clock times (*um fünf*). Option c is real German, but *nach* needs a first event to follow, and there isn't one here.

</details>

19. Two messages arrive while you're writing the shopping list.

```text
Anna: Ich bin im Supermarkt.
Ben: Ich gehe in den Supermarkt.
```

   Who is already there?\
   a. Ben\
   b. Anna and Ben\
   c. Anna\
   d. Neither of them

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Same shop, same preposition, and the article decides everything: *im* is dative and puts Anna inside, *in den* is accusative and leaves Ben on the pavement. Ask Anna for the milk.

</details>

20. A friend texts **Wir sind in der Nähe.** Which description fits?\
   a. Wir sind nicht weit weg.\
   b. Wir sind schon zu Hause.\
   c. Wir fahren gerade los.\
   d. Wir kommen in zwei Wochen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *in der Nähe* is a fixed chunk meaning nearby — dative, because it's a position. Option c is the opposite end of the journey, and option d borrows the time use of *in* that appears in *in zwei Wochen*.

</details>

21. A neighbour says **Im Sommer fahren wir in die Türkei.** What do you learn?\
   a. Sie sind schon in der Türkei.\
   b. Sie fahren später im Jahr dorthin.\
   c. Sie kommen aus der Türkei.\
   d. Sie bleiben zu Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** One sentence, two jobs for *in*: *im Sommer* is a time and stays dative because nothing moves, while *in die Türkei* is a destination and takes the accusative. Option a is what the same sentence would mean with *in der Türkei*.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

22. A learner wants to say "I'm going into the kitchen" and writes **Ich gehe in der Küche.** What has gone wrong?\
   a. *gehen* does not work with *in*.\
   b. *die Küche* does not need an article.\
   c. The sentence is correct.\
   d. The sentence describes a location — for movement into the kitchen, you need *in die Küche*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Nothing here is broken German — that's what makes it hard to catch. The dative just says the walking is happening inside the kitchen. Ask *Wo?* or *Wohin?* before choosing the article and the problem disappears.

</details>

23. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You're at the bus stop, and your flatmate asks where you're off to. You're meeting a friend for the eight o'clock showing.

```text
Ich gehe ___ Kino.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **ins**

**Explanation:** Neuter noun, movement towards it: *in das* → *ins*. Nobody says *in das Kino* in speech, so treat the contraction as the normal form rather than an optional shortcut.

</details>

24. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   A guest asks where the sugar is. It's behind the cupboard door, next to the flour.

```text
Der Zucker ist ___ Schrank. (der Schrank = cupboard)
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **im**

**Explanation:** Masculine noun, and the sugar is sitting there → dative *in dem* → *im*. Gender matters as much as case in this sentence: *in der Schrank* mixes a feminine article into a masculine noun and lands on neither reading.

</details>

25. Put the words in order.

```text
in die / wir / am Samstag / fahren / Stadt
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Wir fahren am Samstag in die Stadt.**

**Explanation:** Verb in second position, time before place. *Am Samstag fahren wir in die Stadt* is equally natural and moves the time to the front. Either way the accusative is what marks it as a trip rather than a description of where you already are.

</details>

26. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Ich bin im Büro.**\
   New situation: it's ten to nine and you're coming through the front door with your coat still on.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich gehe ins Büro.**

**Explanation:** The noun doesn't change at all — only the case does, and the verb follows it. Location becomes destination, so *sein* gives way to a verb of motion and *im* becomes *ins*. *Ich fahre ins Büro* is just as good if you drive there.

</details>
