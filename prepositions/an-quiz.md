## Part A — Against It, or Toward It?

1. A colleague wants to meet this week. Your calendar is full except for the first day.\
   *Ich habe ___ Montag Zeit.*\
   a. um\
   b. am\
   c. in\
   d. auf

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Days take *am*, frozen solid, with no case decision to make. Option a is the substitution English speakers reach for most: *um* belongs to clock times — *um acht* — and never to a weekday.

</details>

2. You walk into a friend's new flat and notice a large photo hanging above the sofa.\
   *Das Bild hängt ___ Wand.*\
   a. an die\
   b. auf der\
   c. auf die\
   d. an der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Something up against a vertical surface is *an*, and hanging there is a position → dative *der*. Option b is the mistake the lesson warns about: *auf* would have the picture lying flat on top of the wall.

</details>

3. Same picture, three hours earlier. You're up a ladder with a hammer in your hand.\
   *Ich hänge das Bild ___ Wand.*\
   a. an der\
   b. auf die\
   c. am\
   d. an die

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Now something is going there → accusative *die*. Option a is the same four words as question 2 and is the commonest error of all here — it would describe a picture that's already hanging, which is not what a hammer suggests.

</details>

4. The café is full, but two seats by the glass are free, so you both head over.\
   *Wir setzen uns ___ Fenster.*\
   a. am\
   b. an die\
   c. ans\
   d. an der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *das Fenster* is neuter and you're moving towards it → *an das* → *ans*. Option a is where you'll be once you've sat down. Options b and d bring a feminine article to a neuter noun.

</details>

5. You're standing on the platform and want to know how long the wait is.\
   *Wann kommt der Zug ___?*\
   a. an\
   b. am\
   c. auf\
   d. in

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** This *an* is the prefix of *ankommen* and has no noun at all. That's why it sits at the very end with no article behind it — the giveaway that you're not looking at a preposition.

</details>

6. Your sister asked for the holiday photos, so you're sending them tonight.\
   *Ich schreibe eine E-Mail ___ Schwester.*\
   a. an meiner\
   b. an meine\
   c. zu meiner\
   d. an meinem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *an* pointed at a person is accusative → *meine Schwester*. Option a is the dative, which would be the location case and has nothing to do here. Option c borrows the preposition you'd use for visiting her in person.

</details>

7. You've arrived twenty minutes early and you're standing by the entrance where you agreed to meet.\
   *Ich warte ___ Bahnhof.*\
   a. an den\
   b. in den\
   c. am\
   d. auf den

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *am Bahnhof* is a fixed chunk for being at the station — dative, because you're standing there rather than heading there. Option d is a real construction with a very different meaning: *warten auf* is "wait for", so you'd be waiting for the station itself to turn up.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

8. Your keys are inside your bag, not visible from the outside. Which sentence fits?\
   a. Der Schlüssel ist in der Tasche.\
   b. Der Schlüssel ist an der Tasche.\
   c. Der Schlüssel ist auf der Tasche.\
   d. Der Schlüssel ist am Tasche.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Three prepositions, three positions: *in* is inside, *auf* is on top, *an* is against the outside. Option d fails twice over — *die Tasche* is feminine, so *am* can't appear in front of it at all.

</details>

9. A learner writes **Ich hänge das Bild an der Wand.**\
   a. Correct.\
   b. Correct, but unusual.\
   c. Incorrect German — here you need *an die Wand*.\
   d. Correct German, but it means something different.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** With a direct object, *hängen* puts something somewhere, so the accusative is the only option. Compare *Das Bild hängt an der Wand*, where nothing is being moved and the dative is right — same verb, different job.

</details>

10. **Er geht ans Fenster.** Which question does that answer?\
   a. Wo?\
   b. Wohin?\
   c. Wann?\
   d. Woher?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *ans* is *an das* — accusative, so a movement. One syllable is doing all of it: change *ans* to *am* and the same sentence answers *Wo?* instead.

</details>

11. In which sentence is *an* **not** a preposition?\
   a. Das Bild hängt an der Wand.\
   b. Wir sitzen am Tisch.\
   c. Der Film fängt um acht an.\
   d. Ich schreibe an meine Schwester.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** It's the prefix of *anfangen*, parked at the end of the main clause. Nothing follows it and no case is involved, which is how you spot the pattern in *ankommen* and *anrufen* too.

</details>

12. The film starts at eight on Saturday. Which sentence is right?\
   a. Der Film ist um Samstag an acht.\
   b. Der Film ist am Samstag am acht.\
   c. Der Film ist an Samstag um acht.\
   d. Der Film ist am Samstag um acht.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Two words, two separate jobs: days take *am*, clock times take *um*. Option c gets the split right but drops the contraction, and *am* is not optional in front of a weekday — the article is already inside it.

</details>

13. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does *an* describe a location?\
   a. Wir sitzen am Tisch.\
   b. Ich stelle den Stuhl an den Tisch.\
   c. Das Bild hängt an der Wand.\
   d. Er steht am Fenster.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** All three answer *Wo?* and all three are dative. Option b is the only one with something moving — *an den Tisch*, accusative — and the verb *stellen* tells you so before the article does.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

14. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Wann hast du Zeit?
B: ___ Wochenende.
```

   a. Um\
   b. In\
   c. An\
   d. Am

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Parts of the week and parts of the day come with *am*, already contracted. Option c drops the article, which German doesn't do here — *an Wochenende* is missing a piece.

</details>

15. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Ich habe jetzt keine Zeit.
B: Kein Problem. Ich ___ dich morgen ___.
```

   a. rufe … an\
   b. rufe … auf\
   c. komme … an\
   d. fange … an

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *anrufen* splits: the conjugated part goes second, the prefix goes last. Options c and d are the other two *an*-verbs from the lesson, and they'd have B arriving you and beginning you respectively.

</details>

16. Complete the dialogue. B is looking around for a table.

```text
A: Wo sollen wir sitzen?
B: ___ Fenster ist es schön hell.
```

   a. Ans\
   b. An die\
   c. Am\
   d. An der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Nobody is moving in B's sentence — she's describing what the spot is like, so dative, and *das Fenster* is neuter → *an dem* → *am*. Option a is the form the walk over there would need.

</details>

17. A friend texts **Der Film fängt um acht an.** What do you learn?\
   a. Der Film beginnt um acht.\
   b. Der Film ist um acht zu Ende.\
   c. Der Zug kommt um acht.\
   d. Der Film dauert acht Stunden.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *anfangen* means to begin, and its prefix is stranded at the end. If you read that final *an* as a preposition you'd spend the rest of the sentence hunting for a noun that never comes.

</details>

18. Compare **Wir sitzen am Tisch** and **Wir setzen uns an den Tisch.** What is the difference?\
   a. There is no difference.\
   b. The first sentence is incorrect.\
   c. The first is future tense; the second is present tense.\
   d. The first says where we are; the second describes moving into a seated position at the table.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Same table, same preposition, and the case carries the whole difference — dative for the position, accusative for the move into it. The verbs shift with it: *sitzen* for being there, *sich setzen* for arriving there.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

19. A learner writes **Ich habe um Montag frei.** What has gone wrong?\
   a. *frei* is at the end.\
   b. Days use *am* here — the correct phrase is *am Montag*.\
   c. *Montag* does not need an article.\
   d. The sentence is correct.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *um* belongs to the clock. Days and parts of the day take *am*, which already contains the article — which is also why option c is looking for a problem that isn't there.

</details>

20. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your flatmate comes home and asks what you did with the photos. They've been hanging above the sofa since this morning.

```text
Die Bilder hängen ___ Wand.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **an der**

**Explanation:** Against a vertical surface → *an*, and hanging there is a position → dative *der*. *auf der Wand* would lay them flat on top of the wall, which is the English "on" leaking through.

</details>

21. Put the words in order.

```text
an den / ich / den Stuhl / stelle / Tisch
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich stelle den Stuhl an den Tisch.**

**Explanation:** Verb in second position, and the chair is being moved, so *an* takes the accusative. With *steht* in place of *stelle* the same sentence would need *am Tisch* — nothing else about it would change.

</details>

22. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Wir sitzen am Fenster.**\
   New situation: you've just walked in, the window seats are free, and you're crossing the room to take them.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Wir setzen uns ans Fenster.**

**Explanation:** A position becomes a move, so *sitzen* gives way to *sich setzen* and *am* to *ans*. *Wir gehen ans Fenster* is also fine if you only mean walking over. What can't stay is *am* — that's the half of the change learners most often forget.

</details>
