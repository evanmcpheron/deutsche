# in

**Type:** Preposition — **two-way** (dative *or* accusative)
**Level:** A1
**Priority:** Tier 1 — very high
**Knowledge goal:** Active

[← um](um.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [Next → auf](auf.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

## Core Idea

*in* means **inside** — inside a room, a building, a city, a country, a period of time.

But unlike *mit*, *zu*, or *bei*, **the case after *in* is not fixed**. It changes with the meaning of the sentence:

> **Location** — the thing is already inside → **dative**
> **Movement toward** — something is going inside → **accusative**

This is the two-way (*Wechselpräposition*) pattern, and it is the most common source of case errors for English speakers, because English marks no difference at all: "in the kitchen" and "into the kitchen" use the same article either way.

Don't study *in* the way you studied *mit*. "*mit* is always dative" is a fact. "*in* depends on the meaning" is a habit, and habits take weeks.

## Location vs. Movement

**The test: ask a question.**

- If the sentence answers **Wo?** ("where is it?") → **dative**
- If the sentence answers **Wohin?** ("where is it going?") → **accusative**

### Pair 1 — the shelf

**Location (dative):**
**Das Buch ist im Regal.**
The book is in the shelf. *(im = in dem)*

**Movement (accusative):**
**Ich stelle das Buch ins Regal.**
I'm putting the book in the shelf. *(ins = in das)*

### Pair 2 — the bed

**Location (dative):**
**Das Kind ist im Bett.**
The child is in bed.

**Movement (accusative):**
**Das Kind geht ins Bett.**
The child is going to bed.

### Pair 3 — the kitchen

**Location (dative):**
**Wir sind in der Küche.**
We're in the kitchen.

**Movement (accusative):**
**Wir gehen in die Küche.**
We're going into the kitchen.

Notice what changes: *der Küche* (dative) → *die Küche* (accusative). The noun is identical; only the article moves.

**Contractions:** *in dem* → **im** (location), *in das* → **ins** (movement). Once you notice that **im** almost always means location and **ins** almost always means movement, a lot of sentences decode themselves.

## Most Important A1 Uses

### 1. Being somewhere (dative)

**Ich bin im Hotel.**
I'm at the hotel.

**Sie arbeitet in einem Büro.**
She works in an office.

**Wir wohnen in Berlin.**
We live in Berlin.

### 2. Going somewhere (accusative)

**Ich gehe ins Kino.**
I'm going to the cinema.

**Wir fahren in die Stadt.**
We're going into town.

**Gehen wir ins Café?**
Shall we go to the café?

### 3. Countries that take an article

Most countries take *nach* for movement — but countries **with** an article take *in*:

**Ich fahre in die Schweiz.** *(movement → accusative)*
**Ich wohne in der Schweiz.** *(location → dative)*

Same for *die Türkei*, *die USA* (*in die USA* / *in den USA*). See [in vs. nach](../comparisons/in-vs-nach.md).

### 4. Time — "in" a period (dative)

**in einer Stunde**
in an hour

**im Sommer / im Juli**
in summer / in July

**in zwei Wochen**
in two weeks

Time expressions with *in* are always dative — nothing is moving.

## Common Chunks

- **im Hotel, im Kino, im Büro, im Bett** — locations
- **ins Kino, ins Bett, ins Café** — destinations
- **in der Stadt** / **in die Stadt** — in town / into town
- **in einer Stunde** — in an hour
- **im Sommer, im Winter, im Juli** — seasons and months

## Pattern Recognition

Read down the two columns and let the shape sink in:

| Wo? (dative) | Wohin? (accusative) |
|---|---|
| Ich bin **im** Kino. | Ich gehe **ins** Kino. |
| Das Buch ist **im** Regal. | Ich stelle das Buch **ins** Regal. |
| Wir sind **in der** Stadt. | Wir fahren **in die** Stadt. |
| Er ist **in der** Küche. | Er geht **in die** Küche. |

## What Changes Without It?

Compare the case, not the word:

**Ich bin in der Schule.** — I'm at school. *(I'm there now)*
**Ich gehe in die Schule.** — I'm going to school. *(on my way — or, as a set phrase, "I attend school")*

One letter of difference in the article, two different situations. English needs "at" vs. "to" to do the same work.

## Common English-Speaker Mistakes

- **Defaulting to one case.** Learners pick dative (because *mit, zu, bei* trained them) and say *Ich gehe in der Stadt* for "I'm going into town." Ask *Wo?* or *Wohin?* first.
- **Using *in* for all "to."** Cities and most countries take **nach**: *nach Berlin*, *nach Italien*. See [in vs. nach](../comparisons/in-vs-nach.md).
- **Using *in* where German wants *zu*.** For people and many everyday errands, it's *zum Arzt*, *zur Post*.
- **Not hearing *im* vs. *ins*.** They're a syllable apart and carry the whole meaning. Train your ear on this pair specifically.

## Active or Passive?

**Active** — but expect the case to stay wobbly for a while. That's normal and not a sign you're doing it wrong. Practise with the *im/ins* pairs above rather than with tables.

## You May Hear

- **Ich bin im Zug.** — I'm on the train.
- **Gehen wir ins Restaurant?** — Shall we go to a restaurant?
- **In zehn Minuten bin ich da.** — I'll be there in ten minutes.
- **Wir wohnen in der Nähe.** — We live nearby.
