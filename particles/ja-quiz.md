## Part A — Plain "Yes" or Particle?

1. Your friend asks **Kommst du mit?** You want to say yes and accept the invitation. Which answer fits?\
   a. Nein. \
   b. Doch. \
   c. Ja, gern. \
   d. Noch.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Here *ja* is the ordinary answer “yes.” It directly answers a positive question and is not a modal particle.

</details>

2. You and your friend both know the bus comes at eight. Your friend starts worrying about the time, and you say **Der Bus kommt ja um acht.** What does particle **ja** add?\
   a. It turns the statement into a question. \
   b. It points to the bus time as shared or already-known information. \
   c. It means that the bus came earlier. \
   d. It contradicts a negative question.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Mid-sentence particle *ja* can appeal to something both people already know: “The bus comes at eight, as you know.” The basic fact remains *Der Bus kommt um acht.*

</details>

3. You look at a menu and are surprised by the price. You say **Das ist ja teuer!** What is **ja** doing?\
   a. It adds a shared-observation or surprise reaction to the statement. \
   b. It answers an unspoken yes/no question. \
   c. It means “again.” \
   d. It makes *teuer* negative.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** This is particle *ja*, not “yes.” In this context it marks the price as something the speaker has just noticed with surprise.

</details>

4. Compare:

```text
Anna ist heute zu Hause.
Anna ist ja heute zu Hause.
```

What changes in the second sentence?\
   a. The second sentence says Anna is not at home. \
   b. The second sentence becomes a yes/no question. \
   c. The factual message stays the same, but the speaker treats it as shared or obvious information. \
   d. The second sentence says Anna is at home again.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Both sentences say that Anna is at home today. Particle *ja* adds the speaker’s perspective: “as you know / after all.”

</details>

5. Someone asks **Trinkst du keinen Kaffee?** You do drink coffee and want to contradict the negative assumption. Which short answer is the clearest?\
   a. Ja. \
   b. Doch. \
   c. Nein. \
   d. Mal.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** German uses *doch* to contradict a negative question. Plain *ja* is not the lesson-taught answer for reversing the negative assumption.

</details>

6. Which sentence contains modal-particle **ja** rather than plain **ja** meaning “yes”?\
   a. Du bist ja müde. \
   b. Ja, gern. \
   c. Ja, klar! \
   d. Ja.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** In *Du bist ja müde*, *ja* sits inside a statement and adds speaker attitude. The other choices use ordinary *ja* as an answer or response.

</details>

## Part B — Shared Knowledge, Position, and Common Chunks

7. You and your friend both know Anna is at home today. Your friend asks whether anyone is there. Which sentence uses the lesson-taught shared-knowledge **ja** naturally?\
   a. Ja, Anna ist zu Hause. \
   b. Anna ist doch zu Hause. \
   c. Anna ist ja zu Hause. \
   d. Anna ist heute zu Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Anna ist ja zu Hause* points back to a fact both speakers can treat as known. The lesson places particle *ja* inside the statement after the verb.

</details>

8. Compare:

```text
Peter arbeitet heute.
Peter arbeitet ja heute.
```

Which description is best?\
   a. The second sentence asks whether Peter works today. \
   b. The second sentence means Peter works again. \
   c. Both say Peter works today; the second presents that as something the listener already knows or accepts. \
   d. Only the second sentence is correct German.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The factual content is the same. Particle *ja* adds the assumption of shared knowledge: “Peter works today, as you know.”

</details>

9. Which sentence uses modal-particle **ja** in the position taught in the lesson?\
   a. Anna ist ja zu Hause. \
   b. Ja, Anna ist zu Hause. \
   c. Anna ist zu Hause. Ja. \
   d. Anna ist doch zu Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** In *Anna ist ja zu Hause*, unstressed *ja* sits inside the statement after the verb. Choice b uses plain *ja* as a separate response, while d uses a different particle.

</details>

10. In the chunk **Na ja ...**, what does **Na ja** usually signal?\
   a. a strong contradiction of a negative question \
   b. hesitation, roughly “well ...” \
   c. an order to stop \
   d. strong agreement meaning “exactly”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The lesson gives *Na ja ...* as a common hesitation chunk, similar to English “well ...”. This is different from the modal particle inside a statement.

</details>

11. You suddenly remember something and say **Ach ja!** Which meaning best matches the chunk?\
   a. “No way!” \
   b. “Not yet!” \
   c. “Exactly!” \
   d. “Oh right!”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Ach ja!* is the lesson-taught chunk for remembering or recognizing something: “Oh right!”

</details>

12. Both people already know the bus comes at eight. Judge this sentence:

```text
Der Bus kommt um acht.
```

Which description is best?\
   a. Correct German, but it is a plain statement and does not itself add the shared-knowledge perspective of particle *ja*. \
   b. Incorrect German because every shared fact needs *ja*. \
   c. Correct only as an answer meaning “yes.” \
   d. It means the bus comes again at eight.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Der Bus kommt um acht* is completely correct. *Der Bus kommt ja um acht* would add the extra “as we both know / after all” perspective; the underlying fact does not change.

</details>

## Part C — Tone, Expectation, and Recognition

13. You enter a café and are surprised that no other customers are there. Which interpretation best fits **Das Café ist ja leer!**?\
   a. The speaker asks whether the café is empty. \
   b. The speaker says the café should close. \
   c. The speaker contradicts a negative question. \
   d. The speaker reacts with surprise to what they can now see.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** In this context, particle *ja* marks a surprised observation: “Wow, the café is empty!” The lesson warns that intonation is important for this use.

</details>

14. Which statement about **Das ist ja toll!** best matches the lesson?\
   a. It always means plain “Yes, that is great.” \
   b. Depending on context and intonation, particle *ja* can mark shared observation or genuine surprise. \
   c. It is only used to contradict negative questions. \
   d. Particle *ja* has exactly one fixed English translation.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The particle does not have one fixed English equivalent. The lesson specifically notes that shared knowledge and surprise can use the same *ja*, with intonation helping distinguish the feeling.

</details>

15. How is modal-particle **ja** normally pronounced in the lesson’s pattern?\
   a. strongly stressed because it means “yes” \
   b. longer than every other word in the sentence \
   c. with stress only when the sentence is negative \
   d. unstressed, with the important stress falling on the word after it

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Particle *ja* is unstressed. That is one clue that it is not the ordinary answer *Ja!* meaning “yes.”

</details>

16. In **Ja, klar!**, which use of **ja** do you have?\
   a. plain “yes” in a common response chunk \
   b. the mid-sentence modal particle \
   c. the word used to contradict a negative question \
   d. a word meaning “still”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Ja, klar!* means “Yes, of course!” Here *ja* is an ordinary affirmative response, not the unstressed particle inside a statement.

</details>

17. Which learning strategy matches the lesson’s A1 guidance?\
   a. Put modal-particle *ja* into most statements so it becomes automatic. \
   b. Avoid ordinary *ja* until A2. \
   c. Use modal-particle *ja* whenever English uses emphasis. \
   d. Use plain *ja* actively, but mainly recognize modal-particle *ja* for now.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Plain *ja* = “yes” is an active A1 word. The modal particle is a passive recognition goal because natural use depends strongly on context and intonation.

</details>

18. Someone says **Anna kommt ja heute.** What assumption is the speaker making?\
   a. The listener certainly does not know it. \
   b. The listener already knows it, so the speaker can treat it as shared information. \
   c. The listener is asking a yes/no question. \
   d. The information is no longer true.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** This is a shared-knowledge use. Particle *ja* signals that the speaker expects the listener already to know that Anna is coming today rather than receive it as completely new information.

</details>

19. Which sentence uses plain **ja** rather than modal-particle **ja** inside a statement?\
   a. Du bist ja müde. \
   b. Anna ist ja zu Hause. \
   c. Ja, das Zimmer ist klein. \
   d. Der Bus kommt ja um acht.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In *Ja, das Zimmer ist klein*, *ja* is a separate affirmative response: “Yes, the room is small.” In the other choices, *ja* sits inside the statement as a modal particle.

</details>

20. **Select all that apply.** Which uses of **ja** are taught in the lesson?\
   a. ordinary **ja** meaning “yes” \
   b. particle **ja** for shared knowledge or shared observation \
   c. particle **ja** in surprise reactions such as **Das ist ja toll!** \
   d. **ja** as the standard way to contradict a negative question

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, c**

**Explanation:** The lesson teaches all three of those uses. To contradict a negative question, it directs you to *doch*, not plain *ja*.

</details>

21. Which short dialogue uses **ja** in the clear A1 way taught by the lesson?\
   a. **A:** Hast du keine Zeit? **B:** Ja, ich habe Zeit. \
   b. **A:** Kommst du mit? **B:** Doch, gern. \
   c. **A:** Trinkst du Kaffee? **B:** Nein, ich trinke Kaffee. \
   d. **A:** Willst du Kaffee? **B:** Ja, gern.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A normal positive question can be answered with plain *ja*: *Ja, gern.* Choice a tries to contradict a negative question, where the lesson calls for *doch*.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

22. In the sentence below, identify which **ja** this is: plain “yes” or the modal particle.

```text
Du sprichst ja Deutsch.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **the modal particle**

**Explanation:** It sits inside a statement and does not answer a question. It frames the information as a shared observation or reaction.

</details>

23. Both speakers already know Anna is at home today. Add exactly one word to give the plain statement the shared-knowledge pattern taught in the lesson.

```text
Anna ist heute zu Hause.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Anna ist ja heute zu Hause.**

**Explanation:** This is controlled practice with the taught pattern. The factual meaning stays “Anna is at home today”; *ja* adds “as we both know / after all.”

</details>

24. Correct the response so that it clearly contradicts the negative question.

```text
A: Trinkst du keinen Tee?
B: Ja, ich trinke Tee.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Doch, ich trinke Tee.**

**Explanation:** The problem is not the sentence *ich trinke Tee*; it is the response word. When you contradict a negative question, the lesson teaches *doch* rather than *ja*.

</details>
