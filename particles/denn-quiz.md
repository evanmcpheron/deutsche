## Part A — Hear the Conversation, Not a Translation

1. Lina says **Ich bin sehr müde.** You want to ask a friendly follow-up. Which reply best fits?\
   a. Denn was machst du? \
   b. Was machst du denn? \
   c. Du machst denn was? \
   d. Ich mache denn was.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Was machst du denn?* makes the question sound engaged and conversational. As a modal particle, *denn* sits inside the question; it does not start the sentence.

</details>

2. Compare these two questions:

```text
Wo ist der Bahnhof?
Wo ist denn der Bahnhof?
```

What does *denn* mainly change?\
   a. The second question asks for a reason. \
   b. The second question asks for a different place. \
   c. The second question sounds more conversational or engaged. \
   d. The second question becomes a statement.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The requested information stays the same. *Denn* changes the conversational feel, often adding interest, warmth, or mild surprise.

</details>

3. A friend says **Ich kann heute nicht kommen.** Which reply is the natural follow-up chunk from the lesson?\
   a. Denn warum nicht? \
   b. Warum denn nicht? \
   c. Ich denn auch nicht. \
   d. Warum nicht, denn ich komme.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Warum denn nicht?* is a common conversational follow-up when someone has just said they cannot or will not do something. Here *denn* adds engaged curiosity.

</details>

4. Someone points to a strange object and asks **Was ist denn das?** What does *denn* contribute here?\
   a. It can add interested surprise: “What is that?” \
   b. It means “because.” \
   c. It makes *das* plural. \
   d. It changes the sentence into a command.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** In this question, *denn* can add curiosity or mild surprise. It does not mean “because”; that is the separate conjunction use.

</details>

5. You ask a new classmate **Wie heißt du?** Which statement is best?\
   a. The question is correct without *denn*; adding *denn* can make it more conversational. \
   b. The question is incorrect unless you add *denn*. \
   c. Adding *denn* changes the question to ask for a reason. \
   d. *Denn* can only be used with *warum*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Questions do not require particle *denn*. *Wie heißt du?* is fully correct; *Wie heißt du denn?* simply adds a more engaged conversational tone.

</details>

6. Which sentence uses particle *denn* in the lesson-taught way?\
   a. Ich bin denn müde. \
   b. Denn wo ist der Bahnhof? \
   c. Wo ist denn der Bahnhof? \
   d. Ich denn bin müde.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Particle *denn* belongs inside a question: *Wo ist denn der Bahnhof?* The statement examples misuse the modal particle, and starting with *Denn* is not the particle pattern taught here.

</details>

7. Your friend says **Ich kann heute nicht arbeiten.** Which reply sounds like an interested follow-up?\
   a. Denn du arbeitest heute? \
   b. Du arbeitest denn heute? \
   c. Warum kannst du denn nicht arbeiten? \
   d. Ich arbeite denn heute.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The question follows directly from what the friend just said, which is a very common setting for particle *denn*. It adds conversational interest rather than changing the factual question.

</details>

## Part B — Particle, Conjunction, and Placement

8. In **Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn ich bin müde**, what job does *denn* have?\
   a. It softens a question. \
   b. It is the conjunction meaning “because.” \
   c. It means “still.” \
   d. It marks a destination.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** This *denn* connects two statements and means “because.” It is not the modal particle used inside questions.

</details>

9. Which pair shows the two different lesson-taught jobs of *denn*?\
   a. **Denn was machst du?** / **Ich denn bin müde.** \
   b. **Ich bin denn müde.** / **Denn wo bist du?** \
   c. **Was denn du machst?** / **Ich bleibe denn müde.** \
   d. **Was machst du denn?** / **Ich bleibe hier, denn ich bin müde.**

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** In the first sentence, *denn* is a modal particle inside a question. In the second, it is the conjunction “because.”

</details>

10. Which sentence has normal word order after conjunction *denn*?\
   a. Ich bleibe hier, denn ich bin müde. \
   b. Ich bleibe hier, denn ich müde bin. \
   c. Ich bleibe hier, denn bin ich müde. \
   d. Ich bleibe hier, denn müde ich bin.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Conjunction *denn* keeps normal main-clause word order: *ich bin müde*. This contrasts with *weil*, which the separate comparison lesson treats more fully.

</details>

11. You hear **Hast du denn Zeit?** Which interpretation best matches the particle?\
   a. “You must have time.” \
   b. “Because you have time.” \
   c. “You have no time.” \
   d. “Do you actually have time?” said with conversational interest.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Here *denn* belongs to the question and adds conversational interest or emphasis. It is not the conjunction “because.”

</details>

12. A learner writes **Denn wie geht es dir?** intending the normal conversational particle. Which correction best matches the lesson?\
   a. Wie denn geht es dir? \
   b. Wie geht denn es dir? \
   c. Wie geht es dir denn? \
   d. Denn geht es dir wie?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Wie geht es dir denn?* puts the particle inside the question. The important A1 pattern is that particle *denn* does not normally lead the sentence.

</details>

13. Intended idea: a neutral question asking where someone is. Sentence: **Wo bist du?** How should you judge it?\
   a. Correct German; adding *denn* would mainly change the conversational tone. \
   b. Incorrect German because *denn* is missing. \
   c. Correct only in writing, not in speech. \
   d. Correct, but it means “Why are you here?”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Wo bist du?* is a normal question. *Denn* is optional and mainly affects how engaged, curious, or conversational the question sounds.

</details>

14. Which sentence does **not** use *denn* as a modal particle?\
   a. Was kostet das denn? \
   b. Wo bist du denn? \
   c. Warum denn nicht? \
   d. Ich bleibe hier, denn ich bin müde.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** In *Ich bleibe hier, denn ich bin müde*, *denn* is the conjunction “because.” In the other three, it is the conversational particle inside a question.

</details>

## Part C — Meaning, Tone, and Natural Follow-Ups

15. Compare:

```text
Was machst du?
Was machst du denn?
```

Which statement is best?\
   a. The second asks what you are doing tomorrow. \
   b. The first is ungrammatical. \
   c. The second means “Why are you doing that?” \
   d. Both ask the same basic question; the second adds conversational interest.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The factual request is still “What are you doing?” *Denn* mainly adds speaker attitude — interest, engagement, or sometimes mild surprise.

</details>

16. Your friend says **Ich esse heute nicht.** Which reply best shows engaged curiosity?\
   a. Ich esse denn auch nicht. \
   b. Warum denn nicht? \
   c. Denn ich esse nicht. \
   d. Warum nicht denn ich?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Warum denn nicht?* is the natural fixed follow-up chunk from the lesson. The particle shows that the question responds to what was just said.

</details>

17. You look at a price and are surprised. Which question can naturally carry that mild surprise?\
   a. Denn was kostet das? \
   b. Was kostet das denn? \
   c. Das kostet denn was? \
   d. Was denn kostet das?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Was kostet das denn?* can sound surprised or especially interested depending on context, while keeping the same basic question about price.

</details>

18. Someone asks **Was ist denn los?** Which simple situation best fits?\
   a. They are explaining why they are late. \
   b. They are asking for a train ticket. \
   c. They are asking what is going on or what is wrong. \
   d. They are saying everything is fine.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Was ist denn los?* is a common chunk meaning “What’s going on?” or “What’s wrong?”

</details>

19. Which description best captures particle *denn* across the lesson?\
   a. It often makes a question feel more conversational, interested, or mildly surprised. \
   b. It always means “because.” \
   c. It changes every question into a request for a reason. \
   d. It is required in all friendly questions.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Particle *denn* is mainly about conversational attitude, not a fixed English translation. Questions can still be correct and friendly without it.

</details>

20. **Select all that apply.** Which sentences use *denn* correctly according to this lesson?\
   a. Warum denn nicht? \
   b. Hast du denn Zeit? \
   c. Ich bleibe hier, denn ich bin müde. \
   d. Denn wo ist der Bahnhof?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, c**

**Explanation:** The first two use particle *denn* inside questions. The third uses conjunction *denn* meaning “because.” The fourth incorrectly puts the conversational particle at the front.

</details>
