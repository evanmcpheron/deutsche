## Mini Review


1. Someone asks **Hast du keinen Hunger?** and you *are* hungry. What do you say?\
   a. Ja.\
   b. Doch.\
   c. Nein.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Doch!* (Yes, I am hungry.)

</details>

2. **Komm doch mit!** compared with **Komm mit!** — what does *doch* add?\
   a. Urgency bordering on an order.\
   b. Warmth and persuasion.\
   c. A question.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** warmth and persuasion; it's an encouraging invitation rather than a bare one.

</details>

3. **Das weißt du doch.** What is the speaker assuming?\
   a. That you don't know.\
   b. That you already know.\
   c. That nobody knows.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** that you already know it; *doch* appeals to shared knowledge.

</details>

4. Why is answering *ja* to a negative question a problem in German?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** Because *ja* would seem to agree with the negative. German reserves *doch* for contradicting a negative, and speakers rely on that distinction.

**Explanation:** *doch* can contradict a negative statement or question, and as a particle it can add encouragement, insistence, or shared expectation.

</details>

5. **Produce it:** Someone says *Du kommst nicht mit* ("you're not coming"), but you are. Contradict them.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Doch, ich komme mit!**

**Explanation:** *doch* can contradict a negative statement or question, and as a particle it can add encouragement, insistence, or shared expectation.

</details>
