## Part A — Contradict the Negative

1. A friend asks **Kommst du nicht mit?** You are coming. What is the clearest answer?\
   a. Nein! \
   b. Ja! \
   c. Doch! \
   d. Noch!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** **Doch!** directly contradicts the negative question: “Yes, I am coming.” This is the discrete A1 rule to learn actively.

</details>

2. Someone says **Du magst keinen Kaffee.** You do like coffee. Which response fits?\
   a. Nein, ich mag Kaffee. \
   b. Ja, ich mag keinen Kaffee. \
   c. Noch, ich mag Kaffee. \
   d. Doch, ich mag Kaffee.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Doch, ich mag Kaffee.* pushes back against the negative statement. Plain *ja* is not the clear contradiction German expects here.

</details>

3. A friend asks **Hast du keine Zeit?** You really do have time. Which answer follows the German rule from the lesson?\
   a. Doch, ich habe Zeit. \
   b. Nein, ich habe Zeit. \
   c. Ja, ich habe keine Zeit. \
   d. Noch, ich habe Zeit.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** When you contradict a negative question, German uses *doch*: *Doch, ich habe Zeit.*

</details>

4. Which situation calls specifically for standalone **Doch!**?\
   a. Someone asks a normal positive question and you simply agree. \
   b. Someone says something negative about you, and you want to contradict it. \
   c. You want to say “maybe.” \
   d. You want to ask where the station is.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Standalone *Doch!* is used to push back against a negative question or statement. That is why it is more precise than plain *ja* in this situation.

</details>

5. Someone says **Du hast keinen Hunger.** You are hungry. Which response is the most natural and unambiguous?\
   a. Doch. \
   b. Nein. \
   c. Ja. \
   d. Denn.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Doch.* directly contradicts the negative statement: “Yes, I am hungry.”

</details>

6. Why can **ja** be a poor answer when you want to contradict a negative question?\
   a. *Ja* is never used in German. \
   b. *Ja* can only answer questions with *wo*. \
   c. *Ja* always means “no.” \
   d. *Ja* can sound as though you are agreeing with the negative; *doch* clearly contradicts it.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The special value of *doch* is that it clearly reverses a negative assumption. English often needs extra emphasis such as “Yes, I do,” but German has a dedicated word.

</details>

7. Your friend asks **Trinkst du keinen Tee?** You do drink tea. Which short exchange is correct?\
   a. — Nein! \
   b. — Doch! \
   c. — Denn! \
   d. — Mal!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The question is negative, and you want to contradict it, so *Doch!* is the correct response.

</details>

## Part B — Encouragement, Reminder, and Protest

8. Compare:

```text
Komm mit!
Komm doch mit!
```

What does *doch* mainly add?\
   a. A past-time meaning. \
   b. A question about coming. \
   c. A warmer, more persuasive nudge. \
   d. A reason for coming.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The basic request remains “Come along,” but *doch* makes it more encouraging or persuasive.

</details>

9. Your friend cannot decide whom to ask for help. Which sentence adds the lesson-taught friendly nudge?\
   a. Frag Anna. \
   b. Anna fragt doch. \
   c. Doch fragst du Anna. \
   d. Frag doch Anna.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Frag doch Anna.* nudges the listener toward an obvious or helpful option: “Why don’t you ask Anna?” The plain *Frag Anna* is possible, but lacks the same persuasive particle effect.

</details>

10. You say **Das weißt du doch.** What are you implying?\
   a. The other person definitely does not know it. \
   b. You are asking a new question. \
   c. You are reminding them of something you expect they already know. \
   d. You are saying you do not know it.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In this use, *doch* appeals to shared knowledge: “You know that.” It pushes against the idea that this information is new or forgotten.

</details>

11. Which situation best fits **Wir haben doch morgen frei.**?\
   a. You are reminding a friend that tomorrow is a day off. \
   b. You are asking whether tomorrow exists. \
   c. You are saying tomorrow is not free. \
   d. You are giving a reason with “because.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Here *doch* works as a reminder based on shared knowledge: “We have tomorrow off, remember.”

</details>

12. Compare:

```text
Das ist nicht so schwer.
Das ist doch nicht so schwer.
```

What changes in the second sentence?\
   a. It becomes a question. \
   b. It means the task is harder. \
   c. It adds a reassuring or slightly impatient “come on” feeling. \
   d. It refers to the past.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The factual claim is still that it is not very hard. *Doch* adds the speaker’s push or attitude — reassuring in one context, mildly impatient in another.

</details>

13. A price surprises you and you protest **Das ist doch teuer!** What does *doch* contribute?\
   a. It marks the sentence as a question. \
   b. It adds pushback or mild protest: “But that is expensive!” \
   c. It means “again.” \
   d. It means “only.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** This *doch* pushes against an expectation or situation and can sound surprised or protesting. It does not change the basic fact that the speaker considers the item expensive.

</details>

14. Which sentence best matches a welcoming formal invitation to sit down?\
   a. Setzen Sie sich nicht. \
   b. Sie setzen sich doch. \
   c. Setzen Sie sich doch. \
   d. Doch setzen Sie sich?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Setzen Sie sich doch.* is the lesson’s formal welcoming pattern. *Doch* makes the invitation warmer and more encouraging.

</details>

## Part C — What Is the Speaker Pushing Against?

15. A friend says **Ich komme nicht mit.** You want to persuade them gently. Which sentence best fits?\
   a. Du kommst nicht mit. \
   b. Komm doch mit! \
   c. Doch ich komme mit. \
   d. Komm denn mit!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Komm doch mit!* pushes gently against the friend’s refusal or hesitation. It keeps the basic invitation but adds persuasion.

</details>

16. Compare **Das weißt du.** and **Das weißt du doch.** Which statement is best?\
   a. Both state that the listener knows; *doch* adds a reminder or shared expectation. \
   b. Only the second sentence is grammatical. \
   c. The second says the listener does not know. \
   d. *Doch* changes the tense.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The factual content remains very similar. *Doch* adds the speaker’s stance: “You know this already / remember?”

</details>

17. Which example shows the **active A1 response rule** rather than a recognition-first particle nuance?\
   a. Kommst du nicht mit? — Doch! \
   b. Komm doch mit! \
   c. Das weißt du doch. \
   d. Das ist doch teuer!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Standalone *Doch!* after a negative question is a discrete response rule you should learn actively. The other examples use modal-particle *doch*, where recognizing the speaker’s attitude is the main A1 goal.

</details>

18. You say **Du bist doch müde.** Which speaker attitude best matches the lesson?\
   a. “I have no idea whether you are tired.” \
   b. “Because you are tired.” \
   c. “You are not tired.” \
   d. “I think you are tired; that is already part of what I assume.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Here *doch* points to an expectation or shared assumption. The speaker treats the listener’s tiredness as something already known or obvious.

</details>

19. Which interpretation best matches **Komm doch mal vorbei.** from the lesson?\
   a. A strict order to arrive immediately. \
   b. A friendly nudge to drop by sometime. \
   c. A statement that someone is already here. \
   d. A question about where someone lives.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The particles can stack: *doch* adds the nudge and *mal* makes the request lighter and conversational. Recognition is enough at this stage.

</details>

20. **Select all that apply.** In which situations is **Doch!** an appropriate short contradiction?\
   a. **Du hast kein Auto.** — You do have a car. \
   b. **Kommst du nicht?** — You are coming. \
   c. **Du trinkst keinen Kaffee.** — You do drink coffee. \
   d. **Trinkst du Kaffee?** — You simply want to answer yes to this positive question.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, c**

**Explanation:** *Doch!* is specifically useful when you contradict a negative statement or question. For the ordinary positive question in d, normal *ja* is enough.

</details>
