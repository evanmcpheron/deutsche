## Part A — Recognize the Conversational Signal

1. You and a classmate are talking about food. Then you become curious about where they live. Which question best sounds like a casual new topic?\
   a. Wo wohnst du? \
   b. Wo wohnst du eigentlich? \
   c. Eigentlich wohnst du wo? \
   d. Wohnst du eigentlich?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Wo wohnst du eigentlich?* turns the question into a friendly aside or topic shift. *Wo wohnst du?* is correct German, but it is more direct.

</details>

2. Compare:

```text
Wo arbeitest du?
Wo arbeitest du eigentlich?
```

What changes in the second question?\
   a. The basic information requested stays the same, but the question sounds more like a casual aside. \
   b. The speaker now asks where the listener used to work. \
   c. The speaker is correcting a wrong answer. \
   d. The question becomes more formal.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** In questions, *eigentlich* often makes the question feel newly thought of or slightly off-topic. It does not change the basic fact being asked about.

</details>

3. A friend says **Ich habe eigentlich keine Zeit.** Which speaker attitude best matches the lesson?\
   a. “I definitely have plenty of time.” \
   b. “I am asking what time it is.” \
   c. “I do not really have time, but there may still be an exception.” \
   d. “I had no time yesterday.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In this statement, *eigentlich* leaves a small gap between the basic situation and what may happen next. The speaker has little or no time, but the answer may not be a completely closed refusal.

</details>

4. Someone asks whether you want coffee. You answer **Eigentlich nicht.** What does that most naturally communicate?\
   a. “Absolutely not.” \
   b. “Not really.” \
   c. “Yes, definitely.” \
   d. “Not yet.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Eigentlich nicht.* is a natural short answer meaning roughly “not really.” It is softer and less final than a flat *Nein.*

</details>

5. Someone asks whether you like the restaurant. You answer **Eigentlich schon.** Which interpretation fits best?\
   a. “No, not at all.” \
   b. “Only sometimes.” \
   c. “Basically yes / yes, in a way.” \
   d. “I do not know yet.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Eigentlich schon.* is a safe, common chunk meaning that the answer is basically yes, often with some qualification left unstated.

</details>

6. Which sentence follows the lesson-taught pattern with **eigentlich** at the beginning of a statement and normal verb-second word order?\
   a. Eigentlich das ist ganz einfach. \
   b. Eigentlich ist das ganz einfach. \
   c. Das eigentlich ist ganz einfach. \
   d. Ist eigentlich das ganz einfach.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** When *eigentlich* starts a statement, the finite verb still comes second: *Eigentlich ist das ganz einfach.*

</details>

7. Which question uses **eigentlich** in the normal lesson-taught position inside the question?\
   a. Eigentlich wie heißt du? \
   b. Wie eigentlich du heißt? \
   c. Wie heißt eigentlich du? \
   d. Wie heißt du eigentlich?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Wie heißt du eigentlich?* matches the common pattern: the question remains normal, with *eigentlich* placed inside it as a conversational particle.

</details>

## Part B — Surface Picture vs. Real Situation

8. The intended effect is a friendly aside. Judge this sentence:

```text
Wo wohnst du?
```

Which description is best?\
   a. Incorrect German. \
   b. Correct and identical in tone to *Wo wohnst du eigentlich?* \
   c. Correct German, but more direct and without the casual-aside signal. \
   d. Correct only in formal writing.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The plain question is fully correct. The difference is conversational perspective: adding *eigentlich* makes it sound more like a friendly question that has just occurred to the speaker.

</details>

9. A problem looks serious at first, but after you check it, you decide it is not very bad. Which interpretation best matches **Das ist eigentlich nicht schlimm.**?\
   a. The problem becomes worse. \
   b. The speaker asks whether the problem is serious. \
   c. The surface impression and the speaker’s real assessment are different. \
   d. The speaker says the problem is already finished.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** In statements, *eigentlich* can correct or qualify the obvious picture: “Actually, it is not that bad.”

</details>

10. Read:

```text
Eigentlich komme ich aus Köln, aber ich wohne in Bonn.
```

What does **eigentlich** help signal here?\
   a. The speaker travels from Bonn to Köln every day. \
   b. The speaker is unsure where Köln is. \
   c. The speaker wants to ask a new question. \
   d. Köln is the speaker’s original connection, while Bonn is the current home.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** This is the lesson’s “originally / strictly speaking” recognition use. The sentence contrasts the speaker’s origin with the current living situation.

</details>

11. Which meaning does **not** belong to the lesson’s uses of **eigentlich**?\
   a. “eventually” \
   b. “actually / in fact” \
   c. a casual “by the way” feeling in questions \
   d. “originally / strictly speaking” in some statements

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** English “eventually” is a false friend here. *Eigentlich* can signal “actually,” a casual conversational aside, or an original/strictly-speaking situation, but not “eventually.”

</details>

12. A café looks expensive from outside. You check the menu and see that the prices are not high. Which sentence best highlights that difference between appearance and reality?\
   a. Das Café ist nicht offen. \
   b. Das Café ist eigentlich nicht teuer. \
   c. Wo ist das Café eigentlich? \
   d. Das Café ist schon billig.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Eigentlich* fits when the first impression and the real situation do not quite match: “It is actually not expensive.”

</details>

13. An English speaker hears **Wie alt bist du eigentlich?** and assumes *eigentlich* must sound strongly corrective, like an annoyed English “actually.” Which judgment is best?\
   a. That reading is too strong; in a question, *eigentlich* is often light, casual, and curious. \
   b. Correct; *eigentlich* always shows irritation. \
   c. Correct; the word makes every question formal. \
   d. Incorrect only because *eigentlich* means “eventually.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The lesson warns against hearing German question *eigentlich* as a hard English “actually.” In questions it is often much lighter and can simply mark a friendly aside.

</details>

## Part C — Tone, Expectation, and Natural Use

14. Which continuation best demonstrates the lesson-taught “door left open” effect of **Ich habe eigentlich keine Zeit.**?\
   a. Das Wetter ist schön. \
   b. Nein. Ich helfe dir nicht. \
   c. Aber ich helfe dir kurz. \
   d. Ich wohne in Berlin.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Eigentlich* often sets up an unstated or stated exception. *Aber ich helfe dir kurz* makes that implied “but” explicit.

</details>

15. A sign says an action is not allowed, but people sometimes do it anyway. Someone says **Eigentlich ist das verboten.** Which interpretation best fits?\
   a. Strictly speaking, the rule says it is forbidden. \
   b. It is definitely allowed. \
   c. The speaker is asking whether it is forbidden. \
   d. The rule begins tomorrow.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** This is the “strictly speaking” recognition use. *Eigentlich* marks the official or underlying situation even when reality may be messier.

</details>

16. Compare:

```text
Wie heißt du?
Wie heißt du eigentlich?
```

If **eigentlich** is removed, what is lost?\
   a. The question word *wie*. \
   b. The present tense. \
   c. The request for the person’s name. \
   d. The casual, newly-thought-of conversational nuance.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Both questions request the same basic information. What disappears is the friendly aside / “come to think of it” quality.

</details>

17. You are already talking about a class project. Then it occurs to you that you do not know where your partner lives. Which question most clearly signals that topic shift?\
   a. Wo wohnst du eigentlich? \
   b. Wo wohnst du nicht? \
   c. Wohnst du in Berlin? \
   d. Wo wohnst du schon?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Eigentlich* is especially common when a new question occurs to the speaker during an existing conversation.

</details>

18. The intended answer is “not really.” Someone answers simply **Nein.** How should you judge it?\
   a. Incorrect German. \
   b. Correct German, but firmer and less qualified than *Eigentlich nicht.* \
   c. Exactly the same nuance as *Eigentlich nicht.* \
   d. Correct only if the question is negative.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Nein* is a normal negative answer. *Eigentlich nicht* is softer: “not really.” This is a useful correct-but-different distinction.

</details>

19. Someone expects a task to be difficult. After looking at it carefully, they say **Eigentlich ist das ganz einfach.** What perspective does **eigentlich** add?\
   a. The task will become difficult later. \
   b. The speaker is asking for help. \
   c. The task is only partly complete. \
   d. The first impression and the speaker’s real conclusion do not match.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The word points to a gap between what seemed true at first and what the speaker now presents as the real situation.

</details>

20. **Select all that apply.** Which uses are taught in the lesson for **eigentlich**?\
   a. A casual aside or new topic in a question. \
   b. A statement that contrasts the surface picture with what is really the case. \
   c. Short answers such as *Eigentlich schon* and *Eigentlich nicht*. \
   d. A word meaning “eventually.”

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, c**

**Explanation:** All three are lesson-taught uses. “Eventually” is specifically identified as a false friend and is not a meaning of *eigentlich*.

</details>
