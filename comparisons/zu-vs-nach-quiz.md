# Quiz: zu vs. nach

[← Back to **zu vs. nach**](zu-vs-nach.md) | [Category Index](README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two words is being tested. Look at the destination and decide what kind of thing it is.

---

## Part A — Sort the Destination

1. Your tooth has been hurting for three days and the appointment is at two.\
   *Ich gehe um zwei ___ Zahnarzt.* (der Zahnarzt = dentist)\
   a. nach\
   b. nach dem\
   c. zum\
   d. in

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** A person or a professional always takes *zu*, and *zu dem* contracts to *zum*. Option b is the trap worth knowing: *nach dem Zahnarzt* means **after** the dentist, so you'd have announced when you're leaving rather than where you're going.

</details>

2. A colleague asks about your summer. You've booked flights to Greece.\
   *Im August fliegen wir ___ Griechenland.*\
   a. zu\
   b. in\
   c. nach\
   d. zum

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Griechenland* is a country that carries no article, and those take bare *nach*. Option b is only right for the short list of countries that do come with an article — *in die Schweiz*, *in die Türkei*.

</details>

3. You're lost in a strange town and stop someone on the pavement.\
   *Entschuldigung, wie komme ich ___ Bahnhof?*\
   a. nach\
   b. zu\
   c. zur\
   d. zum

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A building you're heading for takes *zu*, and *der Bahnhof* is masculine, so *zu dem* → *zum*. Option c is the same idea with the wrong gender: *zur* is for feminine nouns, as in *zur Post*.

</details>

4. Your sister has moved into a new flat and you're visiting on Sunday.\
   a. Ich fahre am Sonntag zu meiner Schwester.\
   b. Ich fahre am Sonntag nach meiner Schwester.\
   c. Ich fahre am Sonntag in meine Schwester.\
   d. Ich fahre am Sonntag zur meiner Schwester.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** People take *zu*, and *zu* takes the dative: *meiner Schwester*. Option d stacks two articles — *zur* already has *der* inside it, so nothing else can follow. Option b is grammatical but means you'll set off after your sister does.

</details>

5. A tourist asks you for the museum. It's the second street on the right.\
   *Gehen Sie hier ___ rechts.*\
   a. zu\
   b. nach\
   c. zum\
   d. in

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Directions and compass points take *nach*: *nach rechts, nach links, nach oben*. No article ever appears in this pattern, which rules out the contraction in c before you even think about meaning.

</details>

6. It's Saturday morning, the milk is gone, and the shop shuts at eight.\
   *Ich gehe schnell ___.*\
   a. zum Supermarkt\
   b. zur Supermarkt\
   c. nach dem Supermarkt\
   d. nach Supermarkt

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Shops and errands are *zu* territory, and *der Supermarkt* is masculine → *zum*. Option d applies the city rule (*nach Berlin*) to an ordinary noun, which German never does; option c says you'll go somewhere once the supermarket is behind you.

</details>

7. A friend has started a job in Vienna and you're taking the train down next month.\
   *Nächsten Monat fahre ich ___.*\
   a. zu Wien\
   b. in Wien\
   c. nach dem Wien\
   d. nach Wien

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A named city takes bare *nach* — no article, no contraction. Option b is real German, but it puts you inside Vienna already, driving around; that's a description of a place, not a destination.

</details>

8. You've run out of stamps.\
   *Ich muss noch ___ Post.* (die Post = post office)\
   a. zum\
   b. nach der\
   c. zur\
   d. nach

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *die Post* is feminine, so *zu der* → *zur*. Notice there's no *gehen* in the sentence: with *müssen* plus a destination, German leaves the verb of motion out. Option a would be right for a masculine or neuter destination.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

9. **Ich gehe nach dem Kurs.** What does this sentence tell you? (der Kurs = the class)\
   a. Der Kurs ist mein Ziel.\
   b. Ich gehe in den Kurs hinein.\
   c. Ich gehe zum Kurs und bleibe dort.\
   d. Ich gehe, wenn der Kurs zu Ende ist.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** With something that has a duration, *nach* is about time, not destination. If the class were where you were heading, you'd need *zum Kurs*. Both sentences are correct German; only one of them answers *Wohin?*

</details>

10. A learner wants to say *I'm going to my grandmother's* and writes **Ich gehe nach meiner Oma.**\
   a. Richtig — genau diese Bedeutung.\
   b. Korrektes Deutsch, aber es heißt etwas anderes.\
   c. Falsches Deutsch.\
   d. Richtig, aber unhöflich.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** The sentence parses perfectly well — it just says you'll go once Grandma has gone. This is why the lesson's one unbreakable rule is worth memorising: people always take *zu*, so *zu meiner Oma*.

</details>

11. Which sentence tells you where somebody **is** rather than where they're heading?\
   a. Ich fahre nach Hamburg.\
   b. Ich bin zu Hause.\
   c. Ich gehe nach Hause.\
   d. Ich gehe zum Arzt.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *zu Hause* is the location half of the frozen pair. The other three are all movement — and note that c and b differ by one preposition, which is the whole distinction in miniature.

</details>

12. **Wir fahren nach Italien** and **Wir fahren zu meinen Eltern.** Why does the preposition change?\
   a. Weil das Ziel einmal ein Land und einmal Personen sind.\
   b. Weil *fahren* zwei Bedeutungen hat.\
   c. Weil *Italien* keinen Artikel hat und *Eltern* einen.\
   d. Weil einmal Bewegung und einmal ein Ort gemeint ist.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The verb is identical and both sentences are movement, so d is out. Option c states a real rule, but the article question decides between *nach* and *in* — it has nothing to say about *zu*. Only the type of destination matters here.

</details>

13. Which of these is **not** possible German?\
   a. Ich gehe zur Schule.\
   b. Ich fahre nach Zürich.\
   c. Ich gehe nach links.\
   d. Ich fahre zu Berlin.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A city name never takes *zu*, however much English "to Berlin" suggests it. The other three each match a row of the lesson's table: a building, a city, a direction.

</details>

14. Which pair of sentences means exactly the same thing?\
   a. Ich gehe nach Hause. / Ich bin zu Hause.\
   b. Wie komme ich zum Markt? / Wie komme ich zu dem Markt?\
   c. Ich fahre nach München. / Ich fahre zu München.\
   d. Ich gehe zum Arzt. / Ich gehe nach dem Arzt.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *zum* is nothing more than the short form of *zu dem*. Everyone says the contraction, but no meaning is added or lost. In the other three pairs the two sentences differ in meaning, or one of them isn't German at all.

</details>

15. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does the gap need *nach*? (der Friseur = hairdresser)\
   a. Ich fliege ___ Japan.\
   b. Ich gehe ___ Friseur.\
   c. Gehen Sie ___ oben.\
   d. Ich komme um sechs ___ Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** An article-free country, a direction, and the movement half of the *Hause* pair. Only b names a person, and people take *zu*: *zum Friseur*. Three different rules all landing on the same word is exactly why sorting destinations beats translating "to".

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

16. Complete the dialogue. B has been feeling ill and has an appointment.

```text
A: Was machst du heute Nachmittag?
B: Ich muss ___ Arzt.
```

   a. nach dem\
   b. zum\
   c. nach\
   d. zur

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *der Arzt* → *zu dem* → *zum*, and *müssen* carries the movement on its own. Option a is a fully formed German phrase, which is what makes it dangerous: it means "after the doctor".

</details>

17. Complete the dialogue. B is going away for the weekend.

```text
A: Fährst du am Freitag weg?
B: Ja, ich fahre ___ Dresden.
```

   a. zu\
   b. zum\
   c. nach\
   d. in

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** A city name, so bare *nach* with nothing in front of it. Option d would need Dresden to be a place you go inside, and option b would need it to be a building.

</details>

18. Complete the dialogue. B has finished work and is leaving.

```text
A: Kommst du noch mit ins Café?
B: Nein, ich gehe jetzt ___.
```

   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. nach dem Haus\
   d. zum Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *gehen* is motion, so the homeward form: *nach Hause*. Option d would be some particular house treated as a building; *nach Hause* means home in the sense of where you live.

</details>

19. Complete the dialogue. A parcel is being delivered this afternoon.

```text
Kurier: Sind Sie heute Nachmittag da?
Sie: Ja, ich bin den ganzen Tag ___.
```

   a. zu Hause\
   b. nach Hause\
   c. zu dem Hause\
   d. nach dem Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *sein* is static, so *zu Hause*. Both expressions are frozen and never take an article, which knocks out c and d on sight — a useful shortcut, because it means you only ever have to choose between two forms.

</details>

20. **Ich gehe nach dem Supermarkt ins Kino.** What is the order of events?\
   a. Kino, dann Supermarkt.\
   b. Supermarkt, dann Kino.\
   c. Beides gleichzeitig.\
   d. Nur ins Kino — der Supermarkt ist zu.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *nach* is doing its time job here: once the supermarket is done, the cinema. The sentence is entirely natural German — it just isn't a way of saying you're going *to* the supermarket, which would be *zum Supermarkt*.

</details>

21. A friend writes **Ich fahre morgen zu meiner Tante nach Hamburg.** What do you know?\
   a. Die Tante wohnt nicht in Hamburg.\
   b. Zwei Fahrten an einem Tag.\
   c. Der Satz ist falsch — *zu* und *nach* gehen nicht zusammen.\
   d. Die Tante wohnt in Hamburg.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Both prepositions are earning their place: *zu* names the person, *nach* names the city she's in. One trip, two pieces of information. Sentences like this are ordinary, which is why the two words aren't really competing — they answer different questions about the same destination.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

22. Three of these destinations take *zu*. Which one does not? (die Apotheke = pharmacy)\
   a. Österreich\
   b. der Bahnhof\
   c. meine Kollegin\
   d. die Apotheke

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** A country with no article takes *nach*: *nach Österreich*. The other three are a building, a person and a shop — *zum Bahnhof*, *zu meiner Kollegin*, *zur Apotheke*.

</details>

23. You get into a taxi and want the airport. What's the most natural thing to say?\
   a. Nach dem Flughafen, bitte.\
   b. Zu Flughafen, bitte.\
   c. Zum Flughafen, bitte.\
   d. In Flughafen, bitte.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *der Flughafen* → *zum*, and short destination phrases like this are used bare, with no verb at all. Option b shows why the contraction matters: *zu* can't sit directly in front of the noun, because the dative article has to be in there somewhere.

</details>

24. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You're at the ticket counter buying two tickets to Salzburg.

```text
Zwei Fahrkarten ___ Salzburg, bitte.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **nach**

**Explanation:** A city, so bare *nach* — and like the taxi phrase, this counter formula needs no verb. *zu Salzburg* would mark you out as a learner faster than almost any other single error.

</details>

25. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You need cash before the shops shut.

```text
Ich muss noch schnell ___ Bank.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **zur**

**Explanation:** A building you're running an errand at → *zu*, and *die Bank* is feminine → *zu der* → *zur*. Getting the destination type right is only half the job; the article decides which contraction you end up with.

</details>

26. Put the words in order.

```text
am Sonntag / zu / fahre / meinen Großeltern / ich
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich fahre am Sonntag zu meinen Großeltern.**

**Explanation:** Verb in second position, time before the destination. *zu* takes the dative, and dative plural is *meinen Großeltern*. *Ich fahre am Sonntag nach meinen Großeltern* would be readable German for "I'll set off after my grandparents do".

</details>

27. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Ich bin zu Hause.**\
   New situation: you're still at the office, it's six o'clock, and you're packing up to leave.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich gehe nach Hause.**

**Explanation:** *Ich fahre nach Hause* and *Ich muss nach Hause* are equally natural, depending on how you travel. What has to change is the preposition: static *sein* becomes motion, so *zu Hause* becomes *nach Hause*. Keeping *zu* here is the single most common version of this mistake.

</details>

28. A friend texts you **Ich komme nach der Arbeit zu dir.** In your own words, what does that tell you?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** She's coming to your place, and she'll come once work is finished.

**Explanation:** Two prepositions, two separate jobs: *nach der Arbeit* is a time (after work), *zu dir* is a destination (a person — you). If you read *nach* as "to" here, you'd expect her at her workplace and never work out where you were supposed to be.

</details>
