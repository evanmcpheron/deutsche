# Quiz: noch vs. wieder

[← Back to **noch vs. wieder**](noch-vs-wieder.md) | [Category Index](README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two words is being tested. Read the situation and work out whether there was a break.

---

## Part A — Which Sentence Fits?

1. It has snowed without a pause since early this morning. It is now evening and the snow is still falling.\
   a. Es schneit wieder.\
   b. Es schneit schon wieder.\
   c. Es schneit nicht mehr.\
   d. Es schneit noch.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** No gap in the whole day, so the situation has simply never ended. *wieder* would commit you to claiming the snow stopped at some point.

</details>

2. Different day. It snowed in the morning, stopped around midday, and started falling again at six.\
   a. Es schneit noch.\
   b. Es schneit wieder.\
   c. Es schneit immer noch.\
   d. Es schneit nicht mehr.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** There was a dry gap, so this is a second episode. Compare question 1: identical weather at six o'clock, different history — and German makes you say which.

</details>

3. Your colleague was off sick for a week, came back to work on Monday, and is off sick again today.\
   a. Er ist noch krank.\
   b. Er ist immer noch krank.\
   c. Er ist wieder krank.\
   d. Er ist noch nicht krank.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** He recovered in between, so the illness returned. Option b would be worse than wrong — it would claim he never got better, which is the opposite of what happened.

</details>

4. A different colleague has been off sick for three weeks and hasn't been in the office once.\
   a. Er ist wieder krank.\
   b. Er ist noch krank.\
   c. Er ist schon wieder krank.\
   d. Er ist nicht mehr krank.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** One unbroken illness → *noch*. If you wanted to comment on how long it has dragged on, *immer noch krank* would add that; plain *noch* just reports it.

</details>

5. You went out for lunch and have just sat back down at your desk.\
   a. Ich bin wieder da.\
   b. Ich bin noch da.\
   c. Ich bin schon da.\
   d. Ich bin noch nicht da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** You left and returned, which is exactly what *wieder da* announces. It's a fixed everyday phrase worth learning whole.

</details>

6. It is half past six. Everyone else has gone home, and a colleague is surprised to find you at your desk.\
   a. Du bist wieder da?\
   b. Du bist schon da?\
   c. Du bist noch da?\
   d. Du bist nicht mehr da?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** You never left, so the working day simply hasn't ended for you. Option a would fit someone who had gone home and come back for a forgotten key.

</details>

7. What is the question to ask yourself when choosing between these two words?\
   a. Ist die Situation angenehm oder nicht?\
   b. Gab es eine Pause oder nicht?\
   c. Ist es Vergangenheit oder Zukunft?\
   d. Ist es formell oder informell?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Draw the timeline in your head and look for a gap. No gap → *noch*; gap → *wieder*. Nothing else about the situation decides it.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

8. Which of **Ich bin wieder hier** and **Ich bin noch hier** means you never left?\
   a. Ich bin wieder hier.\
   b. Beide Sätze.\
   c. Keiner der beiden.\
   d. Ich bin noch hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *noch hier* = one unbroken stay. *wieder hier* means you went away and came back — the English "I'm back."

</details>

9. What is the difference between *immer noch* and *immer wieder*?\
   a. *immer noch* = mehrmals; *immer wieder* = ohne Pause.\
   b. Beide bedeuten dasselbe.\
   c. *immer noch* = eine lange Situation ohne Pause; *immer wieder* = getrennte Ereignisse.\
   d. *immer wieder* ist nur die höfliche Form.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The *noch*/*wieder* difference survives intact when *immer* is added on top. *Er wartet immer noch* is one long wait; *Er kommt immer wieder zu spät* is a string of late arrivals.

</details>

10. You want to say: **The shop is still open.** A learner writes **Das Geschäft ist wieder offen.**\
   a. Falsches Deutsch.\
   b. Richtig — genau diese Bedeutung.\
   c. Richtig, aber unhöflich.\
   d. Korrektes Deutsch, aber es bedeutet etwas anderes.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Nothing is wrong with the German — it just says the shop had closed and has reopened. For "still open" you need *noch offen*.

</details>

11. You didn't catch what someone said in a noisy café.\
   a. Sag das noch mal, bitte.\
   b. Sag das wieder, bitte.\
   c. Sag das immer wieder, bitte.\
   d. Sag das noch, bitte.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** For a repeated utterance German prefers *noch mal*. *wieder* suits a repeated event — falling ill again, coming again — better than saying a sentence twice.

</details>

12. Read the sequence:

```text
Juni: Das Museum ist offen.
Juli: Das Museum ist geschlossen.
August: Das Museum ist offen.
```

   Welcher Satz beschreibt den August?\
   a. Das Museum ist noch offen.\
   b. Das Museum ist wieder offen.\
   c. Das Museum ist schon offen.\
   d. Das Museum ist immer noch offen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** July is the gap, so August is a return to the earlier state. Options a and d both need an unbroken run from June, which the closure rules out.

</details>

13. Read the times:

```text
09:00 — Lena beginnt zu lesen.
11:00 — Lena liest.
13:00 — Lena liest.
```

   Um 13:00. Welcher Satz passt?\
   a. Lena liest wieder.\
   b. Lena liest schon wieder.\
   c. Lena liest noch.\
   d. Lena liest nicht mehr.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Four hours with no break in the timeline. Every option except c requires the reading to have stopped somewhere.

</details>

14. Your flatmate drops a glass — the third one this month.\
   a. Schon wieder!\
   b. Noch mal!\
   c. Noch immer!\
   d. Wieder nicht!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Schon wieder!* is the ready-made exclamation for "not again," with the irritation built in. Plain *Wieder!* would state the repetition without the sigh.

</details>

15. What does *schon* add in **schon wieder**?\
   a. Höflichkeit.\
   b. Eine Frage.\
   c. Nichts — der Satz wird nur länger.\
   d. Ärger — es passiert zu früh und zu oft.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** It is one of the most emotionally loaded chunks at A1. If someone greets you with *Schon wieder du!*, they are teasing at best.

</details>

## Part C — Diagnose and Judge

16. A learner writes **Ich komme morgen noch**, meaning "I'll come again tomorrow." What is wrong?\
   a. *morgen* steht an der falschen Stelle.\
   b. Man braucht *schon* statt *noch*.\
   c. Ein Besuch ist ein neues Ereignis — richtig ist *wieder*.\
   d. Das Verb steht an der falschen Stelle.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Correct: **Ich komme morgen wieder.** A visit ends when you leave, so a second visit needs the word that allows a gap.

</details>

17. A learner writes **Er ist wieder krank seit drei Wochen.** What is wrong with the meaning?\
   a. *wieder* verlangt eine Pause — bei drei Wochen ohne Pause braucht man *noch*.\
   b. *seit* ist hier falsch.\
   c. *drei Wochen* muss am Satzanfang stehen.\
   d. Man braucht *kein* statt *wieder*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *seit drei Wochen* describes one continuous stretch, which contradicts *wieder*. Natural version: **Er ist seit drei Wochen krank** — or *immer noch krank* if you want to sound impatient about it.

</details>

18. Which sentence describes a **state returning**, rather than an event happening a second time?\
   a. Wir treffen uns morgen wieder.\
   b. Sie ruft morgen wieder an.\
   c. Ich komme nächste Woche wieder.\
   d. Alles ist wieder gut.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Things were good, then weren't, and now are again — a condition restored. The other three are repeated actions: a meeting, a phone call, a visit.

</details>

19. **Nie wieder!** Was bedeutet das?\
   a. Noch nie.\
   b. Immer wieder.\
   c. Gerade wieder.\
   d. Kein zweites Mal.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** It happened once and won't happen twice. Option a is *noch nie* — never so far — which says nothing about the future.

</details>

20. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Wie ist das Wetter bei euch?
B: Schlecht. Seit Montag hört es nicht auf. Es regnet ___.
```

   a. wieder\
   b. immer noch\
   c. schon wieder\
   d. nie wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *hört nicht auf* rules out any gap, so the rain is one long unbroken event — and B is clearly fed up, which is what *immer noch* adds over plain *noch*.

</details>

21. You are stepping out of the office for two minutes to post a letter.\
   a. Ich bin gleich wieder da.\
   b. Ich bin gleich noch da.\
   c. Ich bin schon wieder da.\
   d. Ich bin noch nicht da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** You are announcing a return before you've even gone — *gleich wieder da* is the standard phrase. Option c would fit walking back in surprisingly fast.

</details>

22. In which situation would a German speaker **not** use *wieder*?\
   a. Er wartet seit zwei Stunden — er wartet ___.\
   b. Der Laden war zu, jetzt ist er offen — er ist ___ offen.\
   c. Sie war weg und ist zurück — sie ist ___ da.\
   d. Er war gesund, wurde krank, ist jetzt gesund — er ist ___ gesund.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Two hours of unbroken waiting has no gap in it, so that gap needs *noch* (or *immer noch*). The other three all have an interruption written into the situation.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

23. **Select all that apply.** In which situations does German need *wieder* rather than *noch*?\
   a. Der Bus war weg und kommt jetzt zurück.\
   b. Es regnet seit heute Morgen ohne Pause.\
   c. Sie war krank, dann gesund, und ist jetzt krank.\
   d. Das Café war zu und hat jetzt geöffnet.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** Each of those has an explicit interruption. Only b runs straight through with no break, which is *noch* territory.

</details>

24. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your neighbour moved away two years ago and has just moved back into the same building.

```text
Frau Meier wohnt ___ hier.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **wieder**

**Explanation:** She lived here, stopped, and lives here once more. *noch hier* would mean she never moved out at all — a completely different piece of news for the neighbours.

</details>

25. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   It is eleven at night. Your son started his homework at seven and hasn't got up from the desk.

```text
Er arbeitet ___.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **noch**

**Explanation:** Four unbroken hours, so nothing has restarted. *immer noch* is equally natural here and adds a note of "this has gone on far too long," which fits the situation well.

</details>

26. Fill in the missing words. No options given. Two words. *(ausfallen = to be cancelled)*

   Your train has been cancelled for the third time this week, and you are not pleased.

```text
Der Zug fällt ___ ___ aus.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **schon wieder**

**Explanation:** A repeated event plus visible irritation. Plain *wieder* would be the neutral report; *schon wieder* is what someone standing on the platform actually says.

</details>

27. Put the words in order.

```text
da / wieder / gleich / bin / ich
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich bin gleich wieder da.**

**Explanation:** Subject, verb, the time word *gleich*, then *wieder da* as a block at the end. Learn the whole sentence as a chunk rather than assembling it each time.

</details>

28. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Es regnet noch.**\
   New situation: it rained this morning, was completely dry all afternoon, and has started again this evening.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Es regnet wieder.**

**Explanation:** Change only the small word. The dry afternoon is the gap, and a gap is the one thing *noch* cannot describe.

</details>
