# noch vs. wieder

**Compares:** [noch](../words/noch.md) · [wieder](../words/wieder.md)
**Level:** A1 · **Tier 1**

[← erst vs. schon](erst-vs-schon.md) | [Category Index](README.md) | [Next → nicht mehr vs. noch](nicht-mehr-vs-noch.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

## The Simple Explanation

The question is whether there was a **break**.

> **noch** — no break. It never stopped.
> **wieder** — there was a break. It stopped and started again.

English blurs this: "he's sick again" and "he's still sick" feel like variations on a theme. In German they describe genuinely different histories.

## Contrasting Examples

**Er ist noch krank.**
He's still ill. *(one continuous illness)*

**Er ist wieder krank.**
He's ill again. *(he got better, then relapsed)*

---

**Das Geschäft ist noch offen.**
The shop is still open. *(it hasn't closed yet today)*

**Das Geschäft ist wieder offen.**
The shop is open again. *(it was closed, now it's reopened)*

---

**Ich bin noch hier.**
I'm still here. *(never left)*

**Ich bin wieder hier.**
I'm back. *(I left and returned)*

---

**Es regnet noch.**
It's still raining.

**Es regnet wieder.**
It's raining again.

## Rule of Thumb

Draw the timeline in your head. **Was there a gap?**

- No gap → **noch**
- Gap → **wieder**

If you're unsure which is true, *noch* is the safer guess for something that's been going on; *wieder* commits you to claiming there was an interruption.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using *wieder* for "still."** The most common direction of error, because English "again" and "still" both feel like continuation words.
- **Missing the mood of *schon wieder*.** *Schon wieder!* is not neutral — it means "yet again," with irritation.
- **Confusing *immer noch* and *immer wieder*.** *immer noch* = one long unbroken situation. *immer wieder* = repeated separate events. See [immer noch](../patterns/immer-noch.md).
- **Using *wieder* for "say it again."** German prefers **noch mal**: *Sag das noch mal.*
