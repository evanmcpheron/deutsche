# Quiz: zu Hause vs. nach Hause

[← Back to **zu Hause vs. nach Hause**](zu-hause-vs-nach-hause.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two expressions is being tested. Look at the verb and decide whether anything is moving.

---

## Part A — Wo? oder Wohin?

1. It's Sunday morning and you're still in your dressing gown with a coffee.\
   *Ich bin ___.*\
   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. zum Haus\
   d. nach dem Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *sein* is static, so the position form. Options c and d bring an article along, and neither expression ever takes one — which is a useful shortcut, since it leaves you only two forms to choose between.

</details>

2. The meeting has finished and you're picking your bag up by the office door.\
   *Ich fahre jetzt ___.*\
   a. zu Hause\
   b. zum Haus\
   c. zu dem Hause\
   d. nach Hause

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *fahren* is motion, so the homeward form. Option c is worth looking at properly: *zu dem Hause* isn't a thing in German, and it can't be turned into *zu Hause* by adding a definite article back in.

</details>

3. The weather is dreadful and you've called off your plans for the day.\
   *Wir bleiben heute ___.*\
   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. ins Haus\
   d. zu dem Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *bleiben* is static: staying put is a position, not a journey. English gives no help here — "we're staying home" has no preposition at all to copy.

</details>

4. It's eleven at night and your son still isn't back.\
   *Wann kommst du ___?*\
   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. zum Haus\
   d. nach Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *kommen* is motion. Option d is heard often enough in speech, but *nach Hause* is the standard form and the one to write.

</details>

5. You work three days a week from your kitchen table.\
   *Ich arbeite oft ___.*\
   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. nach dem Haus\
   d. zum Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *arbeiten* describes what you do somewhere, not a direction you're heading in — so it belongs with *sein* and *bleiben* on the static side of the table.

</details>

6. Dinner was at your parents' and it's a twenty-minute drive back.\
   *Nach dem Essen fahren wir ___.*\
   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. nach Haus\
   d. zum Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Motion again. Notice this sentence contains *nach* twice over, doing two different jobs: *nach dem Essen* is a time, *nach Hause* is a destination. Same word, and only the phrase around it tells you which.

</details>

7. A friend rings while you're on the train back from work.\
   *Ich bin gerade auf dem Weg ___.* (der Weg = way)\
   a. zu Hause\
   b. zum Hause\
   c. nach dem Haus\
   d. nach Hause

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The verb is *sein*, but nothing is standing still — *auf dem Weg* is movement, and the direction word has to match. This is the case where the verb table on its own would mislead you, so the *Wo?/Wohin?* question is the safer test.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

8. A learner means *I'm going home* and writes **Ich gehe zu Hause.**\
   a. Correct.\
   b. Correct German, but it means something different.\
   c. Incorrect — with movement, use *nach Hause*.\
   d. Correct, but unusual.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The lesson calls this the most common version of the mistake. Unlike most mix-ups in this repository, it doesn't produce a different sentence — *gehen* and *zu Hause* simply refuse to combine.

</details>

9. **Er ist nicht zu Hause.** What does this tell you?\
   a. Er geht gerade weg.\
   b. Er ist im Moment woanders.\
   c. Er kommt später.\
   d. Er wohnt nicht dort.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** A position, negated — that's all. It says nothing about where he's gone or when he'll be back, and nothing about where he lives; that would be *Er wohnt nicht hier*.

</details>

10. Which question does *zu Hause* answer?\
   a. Wohin?\
   b. Woher?\
   c. Wo?\
   d. Wann?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Wo?* is the location question, and the dative-looking *Hause* is a leftover from exactly that. *Wohin?* is answered by *nach Hause* — the same split that governs every two-way preposition in German.

</details>

11. Which sentence describes a journey?\
   a. Zu Hause ist es schöner.\
   b. Wir essen zu Hause.\
   c. Sie arbeitet zu Hause.\
   d. Ich muss nach Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** There's no verb of motion in it at all — *müssen* is enough on its own, because *nach Hause* already supplies the direction. It's one of the most useful ways in German to excuse yourself from an evening.

</details>

12. Which of these is **not** standard German?\
   a. Ich bin zuhause.\
   b. Ich bin zu Hause.\
   c. Ich gehe nach Hause.\
   d. Ich gehe nach dem Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Neither expression ever takes an article. Options a and b are two accepted spellings of the same thing, so if you find yourself choosing between them, you're safe either way — but *nach Hause* stays two words.

</details>

13. **Ich gehe nach Hause** and **Ich gehe nach Hamburg.** What do these two uses of *nach* have in common?\
   a. Both show a destination.\
   b. Both show a time.\
   c. Both need an article.\
   d. Nothing — they are two different words.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Both answer *Wohin?*, and in both the destination goes without an article. Seen that way, *Hause* is behaving like a city name, which makes the frozen expression far less arbitrary than it first appears.

</details>

14. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does the gap need *zu Hause*?\
   a. Heute Abend bleiben wir ___.\
   b. Ich muss um sechs ___.\
   c. Meine Tochter lernt ___.\
   d. Bist du schon ___?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** *bleiben*, *lernen* and *sein* all describe being somewhere. Only b has a direction in it: with *müssen* and no other verb, the sentence is on its way somewhere, so *nach Hause*.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

15. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Wo bist du?
B: Ich bin schon ___.
```

   a. nach Hause\
   b. zum Haus\
   c. zu Hause\
   d. nach Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** A's question word settles it before B has said anything else. *schon* fits neatly here too: B has got there earlier than expected.

</details>

16. Complete the dialogue. B is leaving the party.

```text
A: Bleibst du noch?
B: Nein, ich gehe ___.
```

   a. zu Hause\
   b. zum Haus\
   c. nach Haus\
   d. nach Hause

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *gehen* is motion. Option c would be understood by everyone in the room, but the standard written form keeps the *-e*.

</details>

17. Complete the dialogue. B is describing her working week.

```text
A: Fährst du jeden Tag ins Büro?
B: Nein, montags arbeite ich ___.
```

   a. nach Hause\
   b. nach dem Haus\
   c. zu Hause\
   d. zum Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** A's question is about the journey; B's answer is about the place she's in instead. The two halves of the exchange sit on opposite sides of the *Wo?/Wohin?* line, which is why the preposition changes.

</details>

18. Complete the dialogue. B is on the bus and ten minutes away.

```text
A: Wann bist du da?
B: Ich komme in zehn Minuten ___.
```

   a. nach Hause\
   b. zu Hause\
   c. nach dem Haus\
   d. zum Haus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *kommen* is motion, so the homeward form — even though A asked about the moment of arrival. German looks at the movement in the verb, not at the point where it ends.

</details>

19. A colleague says **Ich muss um fünf nach Hause.** What do you know about five o'clock?\
   a. Um fünf geht er los.\
   b. Um fünf ist er zu Hause.\
   c. Um fünf isst er zu Hause.\
   d. Er weiß es noch nicht.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The time attaches to the leaving, because the sentence is about the journey. To promise he'll be home by then he'd need the position form: *Um fünf bin ich zu Hause*.

</details>

20. You text a friend **Ich bin zu Hause.** She answers **Super, ich komme in zehn Minuten.** What has she understood? (vorbeikommen = to drop by)\
   a. Du gehst gerade weg.\
   b. Du fährst zu ihr.\
   c. Du bist da und sie kann vorbeikommen.\
   d. Du bist noch im Büro.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Your sentence reports a position, which is exactly what a visitor needs to know. *Ich gehe nach Hause* would have told her to wait — you'd still be somewhere between the office and your front door.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

21. Which sentence does **not** describe movement?\
   a. Wann kommst du nach Hause?\
   b. Wir essen heute zu Hause.\
   c. Ich fahre jetzt nach Hause.\
   d. Ich muss nach Hause.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *essen* happens in a place. The other three all answer *Wohin?* — option d most impressively, since it manages it without any verb of motion at all.

</details>

22. Fill in the missing words. No options given.

   Your flat is warm, the shops are shut, and there's nothing you need.

```text
Heute Abend bleibe ich ___.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **zu Hause**

**Explanation:** *bleiben* is static. The one-word spelling *zuhause* is equally correct here, so don't worry about which of the two you've written.

</details>

23. Fill in the missing words. No options given.

   The last train is at eleven and you can't afford to miss it.

```text
Ich muss jetzt ___.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **nach Hause**

**Explanation:** Motion, and *müssen* carries it without help — no *gehen*, no *fahren*. Adding one is fine too (*Ich muss jetzt nach Hause fahren*), but German speakers usually don't bother.

</details>

24. Put the words in order.

```text
Hause / heute / nach / gehe / früh / ich
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich gehe heute früh nach Hause.**

**Explanation:** Verb second, time before the destination, and *nach Hause* stays glued together at the end. Two words, always — this is the half of the pair that never gets written as one.

</details>

25. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Ich gehe nach Hause.**\
   New situation: twenty minutes later. You're through the door, your shoes are off, and a friend rings to ask where you are.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich bin zu Hause.**

**Explanation:** Arriving ends the movement, so the verb and the expression change together. Leave either one of them as it was and the sentence breaks — that pairing is the entire lesson.

</details>

26. A German friend texts you **Ich bin gleich zu Hause.** Is she there yet? What does the sentence tell you?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** Not yet — *gleich* means "in a moment", so she's describing the state she'll be in very shortly.

**Explanation:** German uses the present tense for the near future, and *zu Hause* is still the position word: she's telling you where she's about to be, not describing her journey. For the journey itself she'd have written *Ich bin auf dem Weg nach Hause*.

</details>
