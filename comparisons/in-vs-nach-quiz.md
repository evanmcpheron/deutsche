# Quiz: in vs. nach

[← Back to **in vs. nach**](in-vs-nach.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two words is being tested. Check whether the place comes with an article, then check whether you're moving or standing still.

---

## Part A — Article, or No Article?

1. You've booked a week's walking holiday in Switzerland.\
   *Im Juli fahren wir ___ Schweiz.*\
   a. in die\
   b. nach\
   c. in der\
   d. nach der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *die Schweiz* carries an article, so the preposition is *in* — and because you're travelling there, the article goes into the accusative: *in die Schweiz*. Option d is the mistake the lesson singles out: *nach* and an article never appear together.

</details>

2. Your cousin is flying to Japan for work.\
   *Sie fliegt ___ Japan.*\
   a. in\
   b. in das\
   c. nach\
   d. ins

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Japan* has no article, and article-free countries take bare *nach*. There's nothing for a contraction to contract, which is what makes d impossible.

</details>

3. It's Friday evening and you've got tickets for a film.\
   *Wir gehen heute Abend ___ Kino.*\
   a. nach\
   b. ins\
   c. im\
   d. in dem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** A building you go inside takes *in* plus the accusative, and *in das* → *ins*. Options c and d are the same form written two ways — *im* just is *in dem* — and both put you in your seat already.

</details>

4. You need a jacket and the shops are all in the centre.\
   *Ich gehe heute ___ Stadt.*\
   a. nach der\
   b. in die\
   c. in der\
   d. nach

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *die Stadt* here isn't a name, it's a general place you head into — *in* plus the accusative. Option c is the same words in the dative and would mean you're already wandering around town.

</details>

5. A friend lives in Munich and you're visiting at the weekend.\
   *Am Wochenende fahre ich ___ München.*\
   a. in\
   b. in die\
   c. ins\
   d. nach

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A named city, so bare *nach* — city names don't take articles, whatever the country they're in does.

</details>

6. You've lived in Berlin for two years and someone asks where you live.\
   *Ich wohne ___ Berlin.*\
   a. in\
   b. nach\
   c. ins\
   d. in die

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Plain *in* with no article at all — this is the one job *in Berlin* does correctly. The trouble only starts when learners reach for the same phrase to mean *to* Berlin.

</details>

7. The family holiday is in the mountains this year, and you leave on Saturday.\
   *Wir fahren am Samstag ___ Berge.*\
   a. in die\
   b. nach\
   c. in den\
   d. nach den

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *die Berge* is plural and you're heading into them, so accusative plural *die*. Option c is the dative form with the noun ending missing; the dative describes being there, not going.

</details>

8. Same holiday. You've arrived, and the walking starts tomorrow.\
   *Jetzt sind wir ___ Bergen.*\
   a. in die\
   b. nach den\
   c. in den\
   d. in der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Location → dative, and dative plural takes *den* plus an *-n* on the noun: *in den Bergen*. Questions 7 and 8 are the same holiday two days apart, with the same preposition and only the case moving.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

9. A learner writes **Ich fahre nach der Schweiz.**\
   a. Correct.\
   b. Correct German, but it means something different.\
   c. Correct, but unusual.\
   d. Incorrect German — here you need *in die Schweiz*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** This one isn't a shade of meaning. Once a country brings its article along, *nach* is ruled out entirely and *in* takes over. Compare question 10, where the wrong preposition does produce a real sentence.

</details>

10. **Ich fahre in Berlin.** What does the sentence actually say?\
   a. Ich fahre nach Berlin.\
   b. Ich komme aus Berlin.\
   c. Ich bin schon in Berlin.\
   d. The sentence is incorrect.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Correct German, wrong idea: plain *in* plus a city name is a location, so the sentence describes driving about inside Berlin. Travelling there is *nach Berlin*. This is the error English speakers make most, because "in" feels like the safe choice.

</details>

11. **Ich fahre in die Schweiz** and **Ich wohne in der Schweiz.** What is the difference?\
   a. The first describes movement; the second describes a location.\
   b. The first is present tense; the second is future tense.\
   c. Only the style is different.\
   d. The first is incorrect.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Same preposition, same country, and the case does all the work: accusative for going, dative for being. This is the *Wo?/Wohin?* split that runs through every two-way preposition in German.

</details>

12. Which sentence would you send once you're **already sitting in the cinema**?\
   a. Ich gehe ins Kino.\
   b. Wir fahren ins Kino.\
   c. Ich komme gleich ins Kino.\
   d. Wir sind im Kino.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *im* is *in dem* — dative, so a position. The other three all still have you in motion, option c most deceptively: *gleich* means you haven't arrived.

</details>

13. Which of these countries needs *in* rather than *nach*?\
   a. Italien\
   b. die Niederlande\
   c. Polen\
   d. Norwegen

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** It comes with an article, and the A1 list of those is short enough to memorise outright: *die Schweiz*, *die Türkei*, *die USA*, *die Niederlande*. Treat every other country name as bare *nach*.

</details>

14. A colleague says **Ich fliege in die Türkei.** Which question does that answer?\
   a. Wo?\
   b. Woher?\
   c. Wohin?\
   d. Wie lange?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Accusative after a two-way preposition always answers *Wohin?*. Change one word to *in der Türkei* and the same sentence would be answering *Wo?* instead.

</details>

15. **Select all that apply.** Which sentences describe travelling *to* a place?\
   a. Ich fahre in die USA.\
   b. Ich bin in der Stadt.\
   c. Wir fliegen nach Wien.\
   d. Wir gehen ins Restaurant.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** Two accusatives and one bare *nach* — three different-looking constructions, all destinations. Option b is dative and reports where somebody already is.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

16. Complete the dialogue. B is going away over the holidays.

```text
A: Was machst du in den Ferien?
B: Ich fahre ___ Hamburg.
```

   a. nach\
   b. in\
   c. in die\
   d. ins

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** A city name and nothing else needed. Option b would be the location form and would answer a question A didn't ask.

</details>

17. Complete the dialogue. B needs a jacket.

```text
A: Wohin gehst du?
B: ___ Stadt.
```

   a. Nach der\
   b. In der\
   c. Nach\
   d. In die

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** A's question word settles the case before you even read B's reason: *wohin* forces the accusative. Notice how short a natural German answer can be — no verb at all.

</details>

18. Complete the dialogue. B is phoning from her hotel abroad.

```text
A: Wo bist du gerade?
B: Ich bin ___ Türkei.
```

   a. in die\
   b. in der\
   c. nach der\
   d. nach

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *Wo?* → dative, and *die Türkei* becomes *in der Türkei*. Option a is the form she'd have used a week ago when she was still booking the flight.

</details>

19. Complete the dialogue. B has plans for Saturday.

```text
A: Was macht ihr am Samstag?
B: Wir gehen ___ Schwimmbad. (das Schwimmbad = swimming pool)
```

   a. nach\
   b. im\
   c. ins\
   d. in dem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *das Schwimmbad* is neuter and you go inside it: *in das* → *ins*. Options b and d are one and the same dative form, and both would mean the swimming is already happening.

</details>

20. You are standing in your kitchen. Which sentence can you honestly say? (die Küche = kitchen)\
   a. Ich gehe in die Küche.\
   b. Ich bin in der Küche.\
   c. Ich gehe in der Küche.\
   d. Ich bin in die Küche.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Dative for the room you're standing in. Options c and d are the case swap in both directions and neither is German — the verb and the case have to agree about whether anything is moving.

</details>

21. A friend texts **Ich bin im Supermarkt — brauchst du was?** Where is she? (der Laden = shop)\
   a. Auf dem Weg zum Supermarkt.\
   b. Vor dem Supermarkt.\
   c. Zu Hause.\
   d. Schon im Laden.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *im* is dative, so she's inside, which is exactly why the offer makes sense. On her way there she'd have written *Ich gehe in den Supermarkt* — or more likely *zum Supermarkt*, since for shops and errands German prefers *zu* altogether.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

22. A learner writes **Wir fliegen nach die Türkei.** What has gone wrong?\
   a. *nach* is not used with an article — here you need *in die Türkei*.\
   b. *fliegen* cannot be used with *nach*.\
   c. The article must be *der*.\
   d. *Türkei* does not need an article.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The learner has correctly remembered that *die Türkei* keeps its article, then kept *nach* as well. The two can't share a phrase: the article forces the preposition to become *in*, and once it does, the accusative *die* is already right.

</details>

23. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You're at the airport with a boarding pass for Amsterdam.

```text
Ich fliege ___ Amsterdam.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **nach**

**Explanation:** A named city, so bare *nach* — even though the country around it would need *in die Niederlande*. The article belongs to the country, not to the cities inside it.

</details>

24. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   It's pouring with rain, so you're heading indoors to look at paintings.

```text
Wir gehen ___ Museum. (das Museum = museum)
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **ins**

**Explanation:** Neuter noun, movement inside: *in das* → *ins*. Nobody says *in das Museum* in speech, so the contraction is worth treating as the normal form rather than an optional shortcut.

</details>

25. Fill in the missing words. No options given.

   You're phoning home from your hotel on the Turkish coast.

```text
Ich bin gerade ___ Türkei.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **in der**

**Explanation:** *Wo?* → dative, so *in der Türkei*. There's no contraction for the feminine dative, which means these two words simply have to be said in full.

</details>

26. Put the words in order.

```text
die Schweiz / fahren / im August / wir / in
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Wir fahren im August in die Schweiz.**

**Explanation:** Verb second, time before place. The accusative *die* is doing the real work here — it's the difference between planning the trip and describing where you are once you're there.

</details>

27. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Ich fahre in die Stadt.**\
   New situation: you've arrived, you're standing in front of the town hall, and a friend rings to ask where you are.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich bin in der Stadt.**

**Explanation:** The preposition stays put; the verb and the case both change. *fahren* + accusative is the journey, *sein* + dative is the arrival. If you keep the accusative with *sein*, the sentence stops being German rather than just sounding odd.

</details>
