# Quiz: erst vs. schon

[← Back to **erst vs. schon**](erst-vs-schon.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two words is being tested. Work out which way the speaker is leaning.

---

## Part A — Which Sentence Fits?

1. You are on a two-week holiday. It is day twelve, and you're sad that it's nearly over.\
   a. Wir sind schon zwölf Tage hier.\
   b. Wir sind erst zwölf Tage hier.\
   c. Wir sind nur zwölf Tage hier.\
   d. Wir sind noch zwölf Tage hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Twelve days feels like a lot, and the time has gone faster than you wanted — that's *schon*. Option d would mean twelve days are still to come.

</details>

2. Same holiday, day two, and you're delighted at how much is still ahead.\
   a. Wir sind schon zwei Tage hier.\
   b. Wir sind erst zwei Tage hier.\
   c. Wir sind nicht mehr zwei Tage hier.\
   d. Wir sind wieder zwei Tage hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Barely started — *erst* says the point reached so far is smaller than someone might assume. Compare question 1: the same holiday, the same grammar, opposite ends of the trip.

</details>

3. **Der Zug kommt erst um zehn.** It is nine o'clock. What does that mean for you?\
   a. Nichts — der Zug ist weg.\
   b. Du kannst sofort einsteigen.\
   c. Du musst noch eine Stunde warten.\
   d. Der Zug ist schon um neun gefahren.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst um zehn* means the train does **not** come before ten. There is no *nicht* in the German, but the negative is there — and misreading it can make you miss an hour of your day.

</details>

4. A sign on a shop door reads *Wir haben erst ab neun geöffnet.* It is half past eight and you're standing outside.\
   a. Du kannst hineingehen.\
   b. Der Laden hat gerade geschlossen.\
   c. Der Laden öffnet heute nicht.\
   d. Du musst noch eine halbe Stunde warten.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *erst ab neun* tells you that nine is later than you were hoping — which means you turned up before it. The word reports the opening time *and* where you stand relative to it.

</details>

5. Your usual bus goes at eight, but today an earlier one leaves at half past seven, which suits you perfectly.\
   a. Der Bus fährt erst um halb acht.\
   b. Der Bus fährt schon um halb acht.\
   c. Der Bus fährt nur um halb acht.\
   d. Der Bus fährt noch um halb acht.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *schon um halb acht* = as early as half seven. Option a would flip it into a complaint, as though half seven were an unreasonably late departure.

</details>

6. Which of these words hides a "not" inside it?\
   a. schon\
   b. nur\
   c. erst\
   d. noch

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst um zehn* already means "not before ten." This hidden negative is the single biggest source of misreadings with *erst*, because English needs a visible "not until."

</details>

7. You started a new job three days ago, and a customer is expecting you to know everything.\
   a. Ich arbeite schon drei Tage hier.\
   b. Ich arbeite erst drei Tage hier.\
   c. Ich arbeite nicht mehr hier.\
   d. Ich arbeite wieder hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Three days is very little, and *erst* lowers the customer's expectations for you. Option a would boast about the three days, which is not the effect you want here.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

8. **Es ist erst acht Uhr** and **Es ist schon acht Uhr.** What is the same and what differs?\
   a. The time is different, but the attitude is the same.\
   b. Both are different.\
   c. The time is the same, but the attitude is different.\
   d. Both are the same.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Identical facts, opposite emotional messages. This pair is the clearest illustration in the whole repository of why translation alone doesn't teach these words.

</details>

9. You want to say: **The train doesn't come until ten.** A learner writes **Der Zug kommt schon um zehn.**\
   a. Correct — exactly that meaning.\
   b. Incorrect German.\
   c. Correct, but impolite.\
   d. Correct German, but it says the opposite.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The sentence is fine German — it just means the train comes as early as ten. Swapping in *erst* flips it to "not before ten," which is what you meant.

</details>

10. Why is **Ich habe erst zehn Euro** odd if you simply mean ten euros is all you have?\
   a. Because *erst* cannot be used with money.\
   b. Because *erst* is only used in questions.\
   c. Because *erst* measures progress — it sounds as if more money is still coming.\
   d. Because ten is too small a number.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst* measures how far something has got along a scale that keeps moving. Money in your pocket isn't going to grow while you stand there, so you want *nur zehn Euro*.

</details>

11. Which sentence limits a fixed amount rather than measuring progress?\
   a. Es ist erst neun Uhr.\
   b. Ich habe nur zehn Euro.\
   c. Ich lerne erst zwei Monate Deutsch.\
   d. Wir sind erst am Anfang.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *nur* draws a fence round a quantity: that's all there is. The other three all sit on a scale — a clock, a period of study, a journey — that will keep advancing.

</details>

12. You've been waiting ten minutes and your friend arrives apologising profusely. You want to tell her it's nothing.\
   a. Ich warte schon zehn Minuten.\
   b. Ich warte nicht mehr.\
   c. Ich warte erst zehn Minuten.\
   d. Ich warte noch zehn Minuten.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst zehn Minuten* makes the wait sound short and lets her off the hook. Option d would mean you intend to wait ten more.

</details>

13. Same ten minutes, but this time you're annoyed and want her to know it.\
   a. Ich warte schon zehn Minuten!\
   b. Ich warte erst zehn Minuten!\
   c. Ich warte nur zehn Minuten!\
   d. Ich warte wieder zehn Minuten!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Same ten minutes, same verb — and *schon* turns them into a lot. Questions 12 and 13 are the whole lesson in two sentences.

</details>

14. A learner reads **Das Geschäft öffnet erst um neun** as "the shop opens at nine, first." What have they missed?\
   a. That *erst* here means it is closed before nine.\
   b. That *öffnen* is a separable verb.\
   c. That *um* is wrong here.\
   d. That nine is a time.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** They have read the adverb *erst* as the adjective *erste* ("first in a sequence"). The two are related but do different jobs, and only one of them carries the hidden negative.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

15. Complete the dialogue. B arrived yesterday and feels completely new in town.

```text
A: Wie lange wohnst du hier?
B: ___ seit gestern.
```

   a. Schon\
   b. Nur\
   c. Noch\
   d. Erst

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** One day is barely any progress at all — *erst seit gestern*. It quietly asks the other person not to expect local knowledge yet.

</details>

16. Same question, different person. B has lived here for fifteen years.

```text
A: Wie lange wohnst du hier?
B: ___ seit fünfzehn Jahren.
```

   a. Erst\
   b. Schon\
   c. Noch\
   d. Wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Fifteen years is a long time, and *schon* colours the number that way. Option d would need B to have moved away and come back.

</details>

17. Which sentence would make a German speaker hurry?\
   a. Es ist schon halb acht.\
   b. Es ist erst halb acht.\
   c. Es ist noch nicht halb acht.\
   d. Es ist nur halb acht.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *schon* pushes the time later than expected, which is the whole "get moving" message. For clock time, German reaches for *erst* rather than *nur* when it wants the calming version.

</details>

18. **Erst mal einen Kaffee.** What does this mean?\
   a. Kaffee vor allem anderen.\
   b. Nur einen einzigen Kaffee, nie mehr.\
   c. Noch einen Kaffee dazu.\
   d. Der Kaffee kommt zu spät.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *erst mal* puts one thing ahead of everything else on the list — first of all, for now. It's the sequencing use rather than the "not much so far" use.

</details>

19. In which sentence is **no** opinion attached to the number?\
   a. Es ist schon acht Uhr.\
   b. Es ist erst acht Uhr.\
   c. Ich lerne erst zwei Monate Deutsch.\
   d. Es ist acht Uhr.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The bare sentence is a neutral fact. Both *erst* and *schon* always add a view about whether that number is a lot or a little — neither of them is ever neutral.

</details>

20. Someone asks how far you've got with a long novel. You are on page ten of four hundred.\
   a. Ich bin schon auf Seite zehn.\
   b. Ich bin nicht mehr auf Seite zehn.\
   c. Ich bin wieder auf Seite zehn.\
   d. Ich bin erst auf Seite zehn.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Ten out of four hundred is very little progress along a scale that will keep moving — textbook *erst*. Option a would only work as a joke, or if the book were extremely hard going.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

21. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does *erst* mean "not before"?\
   a. Der Bus fährt erst um acht.\
   b. Erst duschen, dann frühstücken.\
   c. Ich komme erst am Sonntag.\
   d. Das Konzert beginnt erst um neun.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** Each of those denies an earlier time — no bus before eight, no arrival before Sunday, no concert before nine. Option b just puts two actions in order; nothing is being denied.

</details>

22. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You have been learning German for six years and you're rather proud of it.

```text
Ich lerne ___ sechs Jahre Deutsch.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **schon**

**Explanation:** Six years is a substantial stretch, and *schon* presents it that way. Swap in *erst* and you would be apologising for how little you've done.

</details>

23. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your friend is panicking about an eleven o'clock train. It is quarter past nine.

```text
Keine Panik — es ist ___ Viertel nach neun.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **erst**

**Explanation:** *erst* is the reassuring word for clock time: only quarter past nine, plenty of margin. *schon* would have exactly the opposite effect on a panicking friend.

</details>

24. Put the words in order.

```text
um / kommt / erst / zehn / der Zug
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Der Zug kommt erst um zehn.**

**Explanation:** Subject, verb, then *erst* directly in front of the time it is commenting on. Keep *erst um* together — that pairing is where the "not before" meaning lives.

</details>

25. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Wir warten erst eine Stunde.**\
   New situation: you have been waiting an hour, you are furious, and you want the receptionist to hear about it.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Wir warten schon eine Stunde!**

**Explanation:** The hour doesn't change; your view of it does. *erst* forgives the hour, *schon* holds someone responsible for it.

</details>

26. A German friend texts **Ich komme erst um acht.** You had agreed to meet at seven. In your own words, what does this tell you?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** He will **not** be there at seven. Eight is the earliest he'll arrive — you have an hour to fill.

**Explanation:** No *nicht* appears anywhere in the message, but *erst um acht* rules out everything before eight. If you read it as "he's coming at eight, first," you'd turn up an hour early and wonder where he was.

</details>
