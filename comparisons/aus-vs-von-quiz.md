# Quiz: aus vs. von

[← Back to **aus vs. von**](aus-vs-von.md) | [Category Index](README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

Nothing here tells you which of the two words is being tested. Decide whether the sentence is about where something belongs or where it started.

---

## Part A — Origin, or Starting Point?

1. A new colleague asks where you're from. You grew up in Ireland and your family is still there.\
   *Ich komme ___ Irland.*\
   a. aus\
   b. von\
   c. vom\
   d. aus dem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** A country of origin takes *aus*, with no article for the article-free countries. Option b is the most frequent preposition slip at A1 — it would turn your home country into the place today's journey happened to begin.

</details>

2. The bus that stops in your village starts its run at the station in town.\
   *Der Bus kommt ___ Bahnhof.*\
   a. aus\
   b. aus dem\
   c. von\
   d. vom

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The station is where this run begins → *von*, and *von dem* contracts to *vom*. Option b would describe the bus emerging from inside the station building, which isn't what a timetable is telling you.

</details>

3. The doors open and you step down onto the pavement.\
   *Ich steige ___ Bus.*\
   a. aus dem\
   b. vom\
   c. von dem\
   d. aus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Coming out of the inside of a vehicle is *aus* plus the dative. Worth noting: *aus dem* has no short form, so unlike *von dem* → *vom* you always write both words out.

</details>

4. It's your birthday. You open a parcel and there's a card from your father inside.\
   *Das Geschenk ist ___ meinem Vater.*\
   a. aus\
   b. aus dem\
   c. von\
   d. vom

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** A person who gave, sent or said something is a source → *von*. Option d hides a *dem* inside it, and *meinem* is already doing that job — you can't have both.

</details>

5. You pick up a chair in a shop and it's much heavier than it looks.\
   *Der Stuhl ist ___ Holz.* (das Holz = wood)\
   a. von\
   b. vom\
   c. von dem\
   d. aus

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Materials take bare *aus* and no article at all: *aus Holz*, *aus Papier*, *aus Glas*. English "of" pulls learners towards *von* here, which is precisely the trap.

</details>

6. Your train set off in Bremen this morning and you're on your way to Hannover.\
   *Ich fahre ___ Bremen nach Hannover.*\
   a. aus\
   b. aus dem\
   c. zu\
   d. von

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Bremen is only where this particular journey started, so *von* — and the pattern wants its *nach* half to sound finished. Say *Ich komme aus Bremen* instead and you've told everyone you grew up there.

</details>

7. Someone at a party mentions she grew up in Hamburg and moved south at twenty.\
   *Sie kommt ___ Hamburg.*\
   a. von\
   b. aus\
   c. vom\
   d. von der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** A person plus a city name, in the lasting sense, is nearly always *aus*. Compare question 6: the same city name can take *von*, but only when it's a train's starting point rather than somebody's background.

</details>

8. A letter has arrived and the sender is an old school friend.\
   *Der Brief ist ___ meiner Freundin.*\
   a. aus\
   b. von\
   c. aus der\
   d. vom

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** People are sources, not containers. *vom* is the contraction of *von dem*, and *meiner Freundin* is feminine dative with its own possessive, so there's nothing for it to contract with.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

9. **Ich komme aus Köln** and **Ich komme von Köln.** What is the difference?\
   a. Kein Unterschied.\
   b. Das zweite ist falsches Deutsch.\
   c. Das erste ist unhöflich.\
   d. Beim ersten bin ich dort zu Hause, beim zweiten fängt nur die Fahrt dort an.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** One is biography, the other is this afternoon. Both are possible German — which is exactly why the wrong one slips through unnoticed for so long.

</details>

10. A learner means *I'm from Canada* and writes **Ich komme von Kanada.**\
   a. Richtig.\
   b. Falsches Deutsch.\
   c. Richtig, aber unhöflich.\
   d. Korrektes Deutsch, aber es beschreibt nur den Start der Reise.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The sentence is well formed; it just doesn't say what was meant. Nationality and home town always take *aus*, and *Ich komme aus …* is the standard answer to *Woher kommst du?*

</details>

11. Which sentence says what something is **made of**? (das Leder = leather)\
   a. Das Buch ist von meinem Bruder.\
   b. Die Tasche ist aus Leder.\
   c. Der Bus kommt vom Bahnhof.\
   d. Sie kommt aus Wien.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Bare *aus* plus a material. Option d also uses *aus*, but for a person's origin — the same word covering two of the table's rows, which is why sorting by situation beats sorting by translation.

</details>

12. Which preposition marks the person who wrote a letter?\
   a. von\
   b. aus\
   c. vom\
   d. aus dem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** One *von* covers who gave it, who sent it and who wrote it: *ein Brief von meiner Oma*. Option c is the same word with a *dem* attached, which only appears when the noun actually needs a masculine or neuter dative article.

</details>

13. Three of these sentences say what their speaker intends. Which one doesn't?\
   a. Er steigt aus dem Zug.\
   b. Ich komme aus der Arbeit.\
   c. Das Bild ist von meiner Mutter.\
   d. Sie kommt aus der Schweiz.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *aus* treats work as a container you climb out of. For the place today's journey started, German says *von der Arbeit* — a phrase worth learning whole, because it comes up every weekday.

</details>

14. Which sentence contains a contraction that German actually uses?\
   a. Er kommt ausm Haus.\
   b. Sie kommt vonr Arbeit.\
   c. Der Bus kommt vom Bahnhof.\
   d. Der Brief ist vonm Chef.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *von dem* → *vom* is the only short form in this pair. *aus dem* has none, and *von der* has none either — which is why b and d aren't just misspellings but forms that don't exist.

</details>

15. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does the gap need *aus*? (der Anruf = phone call)\
   a. Der Ring ist ___ Gold.\
   b. Ich komme ___ Polen.\
   c. Der Anruf ist ___ meinem Chef.\
   d. Sie steigt ___ dem Auto.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, d**

**Explanation:** A material, a country of origin and getting out of a vehicle — three quite different situations that share one preposition. Only c has a person as its source, and people always take *von*.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Interpretation

16. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Woher kommen Sie?
B: Ich komme ___ Spanien.
```

   a. aus\
   b. von\
   c. vom\
   d. aus dem

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *Woher?* asks about origin, and *Ich komme aus …* is the fixed reply. Learn the question and the answer as a pair and the preposition stops being a decision.

</details>

17. Complete the dialogue. B has just left the office.

```text
A: Kommst du mit essen?
B: Gleich — ich komme gerade ___ Arbeit.
```

   a. aus der\
   b. von der\
   c. aus\
   d. vom

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Today's starting point → *von der Arbeit*. Option d is the masculine and neuter contraction; *die Arbeit* is feminine, so the two words stay separate.

</details>

18. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Wem gehört das Auto da?
B: Das ist das Auto ___ meiner Schwester.
```

   a. von\
   b. aus\
   c. vom\
   d. aus der

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** This is the "of" that means belonging, and at A1 German expresses it with *von* plus the dative. Same word as in questions 4 and 8, doing a third job.

</details>

19. A friend says **Ich komme aus einem kleinen Dorf in Bayern.** (das Dorf = village) What is she telling you?\
   a. Sie fährt gerade dorthin.\
   b. Sie ist heute Morgen dort gestartet.\
   c. Dort ist sie zu Hause.\
   d. Sie arbeitet dort.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *aus* plus a place is the lasting sense: that's where she's from. Option b is what *von* would have meant — this morning's departure point rather than her background.

</details>

20. One speaker, two sentences:

```text
Ich komme aus Wien.
Ich komme gerade von der Uni.
```

   What do they tell you together? (die Uni = university, die Heimatstadt = home town)\
   a. Sie wohnt in Wien und studiert dort.\
   b. Sie fährt jetzt nach Wien.\
   c. Wien ist ihre Heimatstadt, die Uni ist der Start von heute.\
   d. Beide Sätze sagen dasselbe.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** One preposition is about her life, the other about her afternoon — and the verb *kommen* is identical in both. The preposition is the only thing telling you which scale you're on.

</details>

21. At a station you hear **Der Zug von München kommt in zehn Minuten.** What does *von* tell you?\
   a. München ist der Startbahnhof dieses Zuges.\
   b. Der Zug fährt nach München.\
   c. Der Zug gehört der Stadt München.\
   d. Die Leute im Zug kommen alle aus München.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The journey began there. *Der Zug aus München* would be odd in a different way — a train has no home town, and that lasting sense of *aus* belongs to people, not rolling stock.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

22. In which sentence is *von* **not** marking a source or a starting point?\
   a. Das Buch ist von meiner Tante.\
   b. Das Auto von meinem Chef ist neu.\
   c. Ich komme von der Post.\
   d. Der Bus fährt von Bremen nach Hannover.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Here *von* marks belonging — whose car it is. All four sentences are correct German; only the job the preposition is doing changes, which is the strongest argument against learning it as a single English word.

</details>

23. You want to say that your watch is made of silver. Which sentence is natural German? (das Silber = silver)\
   a. Die Uhr ist von Silber.\
   b. Die Uhr ist vom Silber.\
   c. Die Uhr ist aus Silber.\
   d. Die Uhr ist aus dem Silber.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Material takes *aus* with no article whatsoever. Option d isn't ungrammatical in itself, but the article would point at some particular silver you'd already been discussing.

</details>

24. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Someone asks you *Woher kommst du?* You grew up in Poland.

```text
Ich komme ___ Polen.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **aus**

**Explanation:** The one to fix before all others: origin is always *aus*. No article, because *Polen* is one of the many country names that don't take one.

</details>

25. Fill in the missing words. No options given.

   The train has stopped and you're stepping down onto the platform.

```text
Ich steige ___ Zug.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **aus dem**

**Explanation:** Out of the inside of a vehicle → *aus* plus dative. Both words are written in full: this is the pair that has no contraction, however much *vom* next door might suggest otherwise.

</details>

26. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your neighbour has just brought a cake round and you're telling a friend where it came from.

```text
Der Kuchen ist ___ meiner Nachbarin. (die Nachbarin = neighbour)
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **von**

**Explanation:** A person as the giver. *aus meiner Nachbarin* would suggest the cake came out of the inside of her, which is memorable enough to keep the rule in place.

</details>

27. Put the words in order.

```text
nach / der Zug / Berlin / fährt / Hamburg / von
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Der Zug fährt von Berlin nach Hamburg.**

**Explanation:** *von … nach …* is one pattern rather than two separate choices, and a bare *von Berlin* sounds unfinished without its second half. *Der Zug fährt von Hamburg nach Berlin* is equally good German, of course — just a different journey.

</details>

28. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Ich komme aus Berlin.**\
   New situation: you actually grew up in Leipzig. Berlin is only where you caught this morning's train, and you're on your way to Munich.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich fahre von Berlin nach München.**

**Explanation:** *aus* claims Berlin as where you're from; *von* demotes it to today's departure point. If the destination is already clear from the conversation, *Ich komme gerade von Berlin* works too — but the full *von … nach …* is what makes the sentence sound complete on its own.

</details>
