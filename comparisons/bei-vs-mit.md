# bei vs. mit

**Compares:** [bei](../prepositions/bei.md) · [mit](../prepositions/mit.md)
**Level:** A1 · **Tier 1**

## The Simple Explanation

English "with" leans on both of these, which is why they blur.

> **mit** — **together with.** Doing something alongside someone, or using something.
> **bei** — **at someone's place**, or near something. A location, not a companion.

If you could say "along with," it's *mit*. If you could say "at ___'s," it's *bei*.

## Contrasting Examples

**Ich wohne mit meiner Schwester zusammen.**
I live with my sister. *(as flatmates, equals — with wohnen, German adds zusammen)*

**Ich wohne bei meiner Schwester.**
I'm living at my sister's. *(it's her place; I'm staying there)*

---

**Ich arbeite mit Anna.**
I work with Anna. *(she's my colleague)*

**Ich arbeite bei Siemens.**
I work at Siemens. *(my employer)*

---

**Ich gehe mit meinem Freund.**
I'm going with my friend.

**Ich bin bei meinem Freund.**
I'm at my friend's place.

---

**Ich fahre mit dem Zug.**
I'm going by train.

**Ich bin beim Bahnhof.**
I'm near the station.

## Rule of Thumb

- **Accompaniment or means → *mit***
- **Location, especially someone's home or workplace → *bei***

A quick test for the housing question, which comes up constantly: *mit* implies you share the place as equals; *bei* implies you're at someone else's place.

Both take the **dative**, so at least the endings don't change between them.

## Common Mistakes

- ***Ich gehe bei meinem Freund*** *(meaning "with my friend")* → **mit meinem Freund**.
- ***Ich arbeite mit Siemens*** *(meaning "at Siemens")* → **bei Siemens.** *mit Siemens* would mean your company collaborates with them.
- **Using *bei* for movement.** *bei* is static. Going to someone's place is **zu**: *Ich gehe zu meiner Schwester.*
- **Translating "by" mechanically.** "By train" is *mit dem Zug*; "by the station" is *beim Bahnhof*; "written by" is *von*.
