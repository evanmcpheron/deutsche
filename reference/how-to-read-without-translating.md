# How to Read Without Translating

[← Modal Particles Overview](modal-particles-overview.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

The most useful reading skill at A1 is **tolerating incomplete understanding**. This page is short on purpose — the skill is a habit, not a body of knowledge.

## The Habit to Break

Most beginners read German like this:

> *Ich* — I. *bin* — am. *noch* — still? *nicht* — not. *fertig* — finished. So … "I am still not finished"? Why *noch*? Does that mean something else here?

By the end, you've spent thirty seconds and produced a sentence you already understood after four words. Worse, you've trained yourself to stop at every small word — and small words are exactly the ones that don't translate cleanly.

## The Seven Questions

Ask these of a sentence instead. Stop as soon as you have enough.

1. **Who is involved?** *(ich, du, er, wir, a name)*
2. **What is happening?** Find the verb.
3. **When is it happening?** Now, earlier, later?
4. **Is something changing?** Starting, stopping, continuing?
5. **Is it positive or negative?** Look for *nicht* or *kein*.
6. **What do the small words contribute?** Still? Already? Only? Another?
7. **Can I understand the message without translating every word?** Usually yes.

Applied to *Ich bin noch nicht fertig*: I / am / not finished / and *noch* says it's pending. **Not done yet.** Two seconds, no English sentence constructed.

## Why This Matters Especially for Small Words

The words in this repository are the ones most likely to derail word-by-word reading, because they:

- have no stable English equivalent
- often contribute an attitude rather than a fact
- sometimes contribute nothing translatable at all ([mal](../particles/mal.md), [ja](../particles/ja.md))

If your reading method requires every word to resolve into English, these words will stop you every time. If your method is "understand the message," they'll fall into place on their own — which is exactly how native speakers acquired them.

## What "Good Enough" Looks Like

You read: **Wir haben leider kein Zimmer mehr frei.**

You get: *no room available.* That's enough to act on. You don't need to have parsed *leider* ("unfortunately"), *mehr*, or *frei* individually.

You read: **Der Zug fährt erst um zehn.**

You get: *train, ten o'clock, and* erst *means not before.* Enough. Move on.

Understanding the message is the goal. Assigning an English equivalent to every token is not the goal, and often isn't even possible.

## Practical Rules

- **Read to the end of the sentence before deciding you don't understand it.** German saves important words for the end — separable prefixes, verbs in subordinate clauses. Half a German sentence is genuinely not half the meaning.
- **Let one unknown word go.** If you got the gist, keep reading. If the same word blocks you three times, then look it up.
- **Reread rather than dissect.** A second pass at normal speed beats a slow first pass.
- **Read things slightly too easy for you.** Graded readers, children's books, subtitles you've already seen. Volume matters more than difficulty.
- **Say it out loud sometimes.** Small words carry stress patterns your ear learns faster than your eye.

## Where to Get Exposure

- Deutsche Welle's *Nicos Weg* (A1) — free, and small-word heavy by design
- Graded readers at A1
- *Easy German* videos, with German subtitles on
- Menus, signs, timetables, product packaging — genuinely useful, and full of *ab*, *bis*, *nur*, *noch*
- The [phrases](../phrases/README.md) pages here

## The Test

You're doing this right when you can read a paragraph, summarize what it said, and **not be able to say what one particular word meant.**

That's not failure. That's how reading works in your first language too.
