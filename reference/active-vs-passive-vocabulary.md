# Active vs. Passive Vocabulary

[← The A1 Priority System](a1-priority-system.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [Next → Preposition Overview](preposition-overview.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Every word page in this repository says whether you should be **producing** the word or just **understanding** it. This page explains the three labels and why the distinction matters more for small words than for anything else.

## The Three Labels

**Active**
You should practise producing this word. It's frequent, its behaviour is predictable, and using it wrongly costs you little.
*Examples:* [noch](../words/noch.md), [nicht](../words/nicht.md), [zu](../prepositions/zu.md), [mit](../prepositions/mit.md), [kein](../words/kein.md)

**Recognition First**
You should understand common uses when you meet them, but you don't need to produce every meaning yet. Production comes later, usually starting with fixed chunks.
*Examples:* [erst](../words/erst.md), [doch](../particles/doch.md), [mal](../particles/mal.md), [gerade](../words/gerade.md)

**Passive**
You mainly need to recognize the word and not be thrown by it. Producing it early tends to sound odd.
*Examples:* [halt](../particles/halt.md), [eben](../particles/eben.md), [ja](../particles/ja.md) as a particle

## Why the Split Exists

For a noun, understanding and producing are nearly the same skill: if you know *der Bahnhof* means "station," you can say it.

Small words don't work that way. You can understand *Das ist eben so* perfectly — resigned acceptance, no complaint intended — without having any idea when it would be appropriate for **you** to say it. Comprehension needs only the meaning. Production needs the social conditions too.

That gap is why "learn 20 words a day" advice falls apart here.

## The Real Risk of Producing Too Early

Getting a preposition wrong is harmless — *Ich gehe nach dem Arzt* is odd, and everyone still knows where you're going.

Getting a **particle** wrong lands differently, because particles carry attitude:

- *doch* misplaced can sound argumentative
- *ja* misplaced can sound like you're assuming things about the listener
- *eben* or *halt* misplaced can sound dismissive of what someone just said

None of these are disasters, and no German speaker will hold them against a learner. But they're a reason not to rush — and a reason the ordering in this repository puts particles last.

## How a Word Moves from Passive to Active

The progression that actually happens:

1. **You stop noticing it.** It's just background noise in the sentence.
2. **You notice it and find it annoying.** Why is that word even there?
3. **You notice its absence.** A sentence without it starts to sound blunt.
4. **You use it by accident,** correctly, and only realize afterwards.
5. **You use it on purpose.**

Step 4 is the goal, and you can't force it. You can only feed it — exposure, exposure, exposure. That's what the [phrases](../phrases/README.md) pages are for.

## What to Do with Each Label

| Label | Your job |
|---|---|
| **Active** | Say the examples aloud. Vary them. Use the word today. |
| **Recognition First** | Read the examples. Learn any fixed chunks whole. Don't build your own sentences with it yet. |
| **Passive** | Read the page once. Understand the effect. Move on. |

## A Practical Test

Before producing a small word you're unsure about, ask: **have I actually heard a German speaker use it in this exact kind of sentence?**

If yes, copy them. If no, use the simpler version. *Kannst du mir helfen?* is always fine; *Kannst du mir mal helfen?* is better only if you've heard it enough to feel it.
