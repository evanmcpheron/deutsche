# Quiz: wieder

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

## Part A — Read the Situation

1. Anna spent June in Italy, came home in July, and is flying to Italy in September.\
   a. Anna ist noch in Italien.\
   b. Anna ist immer in Italien.\
   c. Anna fährt wieder nach Italien.\
   d. Anna fährt schon nach Italien.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The trip happened, ended, and is happening a second time — that is *wieder*. *noch* would claim she never came home.

</details>

2. **Ich bin wieder da.** Was ist passiert?\
   a. Ich bin nie weggegangen.\
   b. Ich komme später.\
   c. Ich war weg und bin zurück.\
   d. Ich gehe gleich.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *wieder da* is the everyday way to announce a return. Option a is the *noch* reading: *Ich bin noch da* would mean you never left.

</details>

3. Look at the months:

```text
Mai: Der Laden ist offen.
Juni: Der Laden ist geschlossen.
Juli: Der Laden ist offen.
```

   Welcher Satz beschreibt den Juli?\
   a. Der Laden ist noch offen.\
   b. Der Laden ist wieder offen.\
   c. Der Laden ist schon offen.\
   d. Der Laden ist immer offen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** There is a gap in June, so the July state is a return, not a continuation. *noch offen* would need the shop to have stayed open right through.

</details>

4. You step out of a meeting to fetch a coffee and want to tell your colleagues you'll be back in a moment.\
   a. Ich bin noch da.\
   b. Ich bin schon wieder da.\
   c. Ich bin gleich wieder da.\
   d. Ich bin nie wieder da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *Ich bin gleich wieder da* is the standard phrase for "back in a second." Option b would suit someone commenting that you have returned suspiciously fast.

</details>

5. Your flatmate has arrived late three evenings this week. Tonight he walks in late again.\
   a. Du bist noch zu spät.\
   b. Du bist erst zu spät.\
   c. Du bist immer noch zu spät.\
   d. Du bist schon wieder zu spät.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *schon wieder* carries the irritation that fits a fourth late evening. Plain *wieder* would state the repetition without the sigh.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

6. What is the difference between *immer noch* and *immer wieder*?\
   a. *immer noch* = mehrmals, *immer wieder* = ohne Pause.\
   b. *immer noch* = ohne Pause, *immer wieder* = mehrmals.\
   c. Beide bedeuten dasselbe.\
   d. *immer wieder* ist nur die höfliche Form.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *immer noch* is one unbroken situation that has lasted longer than expected. *immer wieder* is a series of separate events that keep coming back. The difference is exactly the *noch* / *wieder* difference, with *immer* on top.

</details>

7. You didn't catch what someone said and want them to repeat it.\
   a. Sag das noch mal, bitte.\
   b. Sag das wieder, bitte.\
   c. Sag das immer wieder, bitte.\
   d. Sag das schon wieder, bitte.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** For a repeated utterance German prefers *noch mal*. *wieder* fits a repeated event — coming again, falling ill again — better than a repeated sentence.

</details>

8. You want to say: **He's still ill.** A learner writes **Er ist wieder krank.** How would you judge it?\
   a. Falsches Deutsch.\
   b. Richtig — genau diese Bedeutung.\
   c. Richtig, aber unhöflich.\
   d. Korrektes Deutsch, aber es bedeutet etwas anderes.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** The German is fine; it just describes a second illness after a recovery. For one unbroken illness you need **Er ist noch krank.**

</details>

9. It has rained without a break since Monday. It is Thursday and the rain is still falling.\
   a. Es regnet wieder.\
   b. Es regnet schon wieder.\
   c. Es regnet immer noch.\
   d. Es regnet nie wieder.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** No gap, and it has gone on longer than anyone wanted — *immer noch*. Options a and b both require the rain to have stopped at some point.

</details>

10. **Nie wieder!** Wann sagt man das?\
   a. Wenn etwas gerade beginnt.\
   b. Wenn man etwas noch nicht gemacht hat.\
   c. Wenn man etwas gleich wiederholt.\
   d. Wenn man etwas nicht noch einmal machen will.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *nie wieder* = never again: it happened once, and that was enough. Option b is *noch nie* — never so far — which is a very different sentence.

</details>

11. Which sentence does **not** describe a return to an earlier state?\
   a. Alles ist wieder gut.\
   b. Wir treffen uns morgen wieder.\
   c. Er ist wieder gesund.\
   d. Das Wetter ist wieder schön.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** In b, *wieder* marks a repeated event — another meeting. The others restore a condition that had been true before and then stopped. Both are normal uses of *wieder*; they just work on different things.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Judgement

12. Complete the dialogue. B's father was ill last month and has recovered.

```text
A: Wie geht es deinem Vater?
B: Danke, es geht ihm ___ gut.
```

   a. schon\
   b. noch\
   c. erst\
   d. wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** He was well, then ill, and is well once more — a textbook return to an earlier state. *noch* would mean the good health never lapsed, which contradicts the situation.

</details>

13. A learner wants to say "I'll come again tomorrow" and writes **Ich komme morgen noch.** What went wrong?\
   a. *noch* sagt, dass etwas nicht aufgehört hat — hier braucht man *wieder*.\
   b. Das Verb steht an der falschen Stelle.\
   c. *morgen* darf nicht in der Satzmitte stehen.\
   d. Man braucht *kein* statt *noch*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Correct: **Ich komme morgen wieder.** A visit is a separate event that ends and happens again, so *wieder* is the word. This *noch*/*wieder* mix-up is the most common single error with both words.

</details>

14. Read the sequence:

```text
Montag: Das Café ist geschlossen.
Dienstag: Das Café ist geschlossen.
Mittwoch: Das Café öffnet.
```

   Welcher Satz passt zum Mittwoch?\
   a. Das Café ist noch geschlossen.\
   b. Das Café ist schon offen.\
   c. Das Café ist wieder offen.\
   d. Das Café ist immer offen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** Two closed days, then open again — the earlier state has returned. *schon offen* would be the sentence if it opened surprisingly early on a normal morning.

</details>

15. Which sentence says that the illness never stopped?\
   a. Er ist noch krank.\
   b. Er ist wieder krank.\
   c. Er ist schon wieder krank.\
   d. Er war krank.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *noch* means the state has run without interruption. Both b and c need a recovery in the middle, and d places the whole thing in the past.

</details>

16. You tell a friend you are moving flat, and she replies: **Schon wieder?** What is her tone?\
   a. Begeistert.\
   b. Leicht genervt oder überrascht.\
   c. Traurig.\
   d. Ganz neutral, ohne Gefühl.\

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *schon wieder* is one of the most emotionally loaded chunks at A1 — it says "again, and too soon." A neutral comment would be *Wieder?* or *Noch mal?*

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

17. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does *wieder* mean "back" — a state returning to how it was?\
   a. Ich bin wieder da.\
   b. Alles ist wieder gut.\
   c. Wir sehen uns wieder.\
   d. Er ist wieder gesund.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, b, d**

**Explanation:** Presence, wellbeing and health are all conditions being restored. In c, *wieder* marks a repeated event — a future meeting — rather than a state coming back.

</details>

18. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your laptop stopped working, you took it to a repair shop, and it now works.

```text
Mein Laptop funktioniert ___.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **wieder**

**Explanation:** It worked, it stopped, it works once more. *noch* here would mean it had never broken down in the first place.

</details>

19. Put the words in order to say you'll be right back.

```text
da / gleich / bin / wieder / ich
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ich bin gleich wieder da.**

**Explanation:** Subject, verb, then the time word *gleich*, then *wieder da* as a unit at the end. Learn the whole sentence as a chunk — you will use it constantly.

</details>

20. **Produce it:** You had a cold last week and are healthy now. A friend asks how you are.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Es geht mir wieder gut.**

**Explanation:** *Ich bin wieder gesund* is equally natural, and *Danke, wieder gut!* works in quick conversation. What matters is the *wieder*: without it you would just be reporting that you are well, with no sense that you had been ill.

</details>
