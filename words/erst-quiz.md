# Quiz: erst

[← Back to **erst**](erst.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

*erst* is a **Recognition First** word, so most of this quiz is about reading it correctly rather than producing it.

---

## Part A — Read the Situation

1. The film starts at nine. It is seven, your friend is already putting his coat on, and you want to tell him there is no rush.

```text
Es ist ___ sieben Uhr.
```

   a. erst\
   b. schon\
   c. wieder\
   d. nicht mehr

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *erst* says the point reached so far is smaller than the listener assumes — here, "only seven, relax." *schon sieben* would be the panic version.

</details>

2. **Der Zug kommt erst um zehn.** What does this mean?\
   a. Der Zug kommt vor zehn.\
   b. Der erste Zug fährt um zehn.\
   c. Der Zug kommt nicht vor zehn.\
   d. Der Zug ist um zehn schon gekommen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** There is no visible *nicht* in the German, but *erst um zehn* already means "not before ten." Option b is the classic English-speaker misreading — treating *erst* as the adjective *erste*.

</details>

3. You are standing outside a shop at half past eight. The sign on the door says it opens at nine.\
   a. Das Geschäft öffnet schon um neun.\
   b. Das Geschäft ist noch offen.\
   c. Das Geschäft öffnet wieder um neun.\
   d. Das Geschäft öffnet erst um neun.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** From where you are standing, nine is later than you want — *erst* carries that. *schon um neun* would suggest nine is impressively early.

</details>

4. Complete the dialogue. B still feels like a complete beginner.

```text
A: Wie lange lernst du schon Deutsch?
B: ___ zwei Monate.
```

   a. Schon\
   b. Noch\
   c. Wieder\
   d. Erst

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *erst* makes the two months sound like very little progress so far. *Schon zwei Monate* would be the proud version, treating the same number as a lot.

</details>

5. You come home tired and hungry. Your partner suggests cleaning the kitchen. You want food before anything else.\
   a. Erst essen, dann putzen.\
   b. Noch essen, dann putzen.\
   c. Wieder essen, dann putzen.\
   d. Schon essen, dann putzen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** This is the "first, before anything else" use — the same underlying idea, since eating is the earliest point on the sequence. *zuerst essen* is also correct and slightly more formal.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

6. **Es ist erst acht Uhr** and **Es ist schon acht Uhr** report the same time. What actually differs?\
   a. The speaker's attitude toward the time.\
   b. The time itself.\
   c. The place.\
   d. The person who is speaking.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** Identical facts, opposite messages: one says "we have time," the other says "hurry up." This pair is the clearest example in the whole repository of why translation alone doesn't teach these words.

</details>

7. **Ich komme erst morgen.** Which statement is correct?\
   a. Ich komme heute und morgen.\
   b. Ich komme heute nicht, sondern morgen.\
   c. Ich komme morgen als Erster.\
   d. Ich bin morgen schon da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *erst morgen* contains a hidden negative: not today. Option c reads *erst* as "first in a queue," which is a different word (*der Erste*).

</details>

8. Why does German say **Ich habe nur zehn Euro** rather than *erst zehn Euro*?\
   a. Because *erst* is never used with numbers.\
   b. Because *nur* is more polite.\
   c. Because money here is a fixed amount, not progress along something that keeps moving.\
   d. Because *erst* is used only in questions.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *nur* limits a fixed amount; *erst* measures progress along something that keeps moving, like a clock or a period of study. Ten euros in your pocket isn't going to grow while you stand there.

</details>

9. You want to say: **We've been waiting a whole hour!** A learner writes **Wir warten erst eine Stunde.** How would you judge it?\
   a. Correct German, but it says the opposite.\
   b. Correct — exactly that meaning.\
   c. Incorrect German.\
   d. Correct, but impolite.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The sentence is fine German, but *erst* makes the hour sound short — "only an hour so far." For the complaint you want **Wir warten schon eine Stunde.**

</details>

10. In which sentence does *erst* **not** mean "less far along than you'd expect"?\
   a. Es ist erst sieben Uhr.\
   b. Ich muss erst duschen.\
   c. Ich bin erst seit Montag hier.\
   d. Wir sind erst am Anfang.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** In b, *erst* orders two actions — shower first, then everything else. The other three measure how far along a scale something has got.

</details>

11. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Sollen wir gleich losgehen?
B: ___ mal einen Kaffee!
```

   a. Noch\
   b. Schon\
   c. Wieder\
   d. Erst

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *erst mal* = first of all, for now. It is one of the most common spoken chunks with *erst*, and it puts one thing ahead of everything else on the list.

</details>

## Part C — Interpret and Diagnose

12. The last bus is at eleven. It is ten, your friend is anxious about missing it, and you want to say there is no hurry.\
   a. Der Bus fährt schon um elf.\
   b. Der Bus fährt erst um elf.\
   c. Der Bus fährt noch um elf.\
   d. Der Bus fährt wieder um elf.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *erst um elf* = not before eleven, so there is an hour in hand. Note how the same sentence would sound impatient to someone who wanted to be home early — *erst* reports the gap, and the situation supplies the mood.

</details>

13. A learner writes **Ich bin erste seit gestern hier.** What is wrong?\
   a. *seit* is wrong here.\
   b. *hier* is in the wrong position.\
   c. You need *schon* instead of *erst*.\
   d. *erste* is an adjective — here you need the adverb *erst*.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Correct: **Ich bin erst seit gestern hier.** *erste* belongs in front of a noun (*der erste Tag*); the little adverb *erst* has no ending at all.

</details>

14. A shop assistant tells you: **Wir haben erst ab neun geöffnet.** What is she assuming about you?\
   a. That you are arriving too late.\
   b. That you are there before the shop opens.\
   c. That you do not want to buy anything.\
   d. That you were already there yesterday.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *erst ab neun* tells you that nine is later than you were hoping — so you must have turned up before it. The word does a lot of work: it reports the time *and* your position relative to it.

</details>

15. You are two days into a two-week holiday. A friend asks how it is going, and you want to say the holiday has barely started.\
   a. Wir sind schon zwei Tage hier.\
   b. Wir sind noch zwei Tage hier.\
   c. Wir sind erst zwei Tage hier.\
   d. Wir sind wieder zwei Tage hier.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst zwei Tage* = only two days so far, with plenty to come. Option a is good German too, but it treats two days as a lot — the opposite of what you mean. Option b would say two days are left.

</details>

16. **Erst mal langsam!** What does this mean approximately?\
   a. Fahr schneller.\
   b. Mach es noch einmal.\
   c. Nicht so schnell — eins nach dem anderen.\
   d. Wir haben keine Zeit.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *erst mal* puts one thing before the rest, so the phrase asks the other person to slow down and take things in order.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Apply

17. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences does *erst* carry a hidden "not"?\
   a. Der Bus fährt erst um acht.\
   b. Erst essen, dann arbeiten.\
   c. Das Geschäft öffnet erst um neun.\
   d. Ich komme erst morgen.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** Each of those means "not before" a point in time. In b, nothing is being denied — the sentence simply puts eating ahead of working in a sequence.

</details>

18. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   Your alarm is set for seven. You wake up, look at the clock, see half past six, and are pleased you can go back to sleep.

```text
Es ist ___ halb sieben.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **erst**

**Explanation:** *Es ist erst halb sieben.* — only half six, there's time left. Note that German *halb sieben* is 6:30, not 7:30: it counts towards the coming hour.

</details>

19. **Das kommt erst später.** In your own words, what does that tell you about the timing?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** It will **not** happen now — it happens at some later point, later than you might have hoped or assumed.

**Explanation:** The information in *erst* is the gap between now and the real time. Someone saying this is usually managing your expectations: don't wait for it yet.

</details>

20. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Es ist erst acht Uhr.**\
   New situation: your train left at five to eight and you are standing on an empty platform.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Es ist schon acht Uhr.**

**Explanation:** Change one word and the whole message flips from "plenty of time" to "too late." The clock is identical in both sentences — only your relationship to it has changed.

</details>
