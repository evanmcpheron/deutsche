# Quiz: schon

[← Back to **schon**](schon.md) | [Category Index](./README.md) | [↑ Master Index](../MASTER_INDEX.md)

Try each question before opening its solution. Each solution is directly below the question so you can check your understanding as you go.

---

## Part A — Read the Situation

1. Your train leaves at 8:05. You expected it to be about twenty to eight, you look at your watch, and you get a shock.

```text
Es ist ___ acht Uhr!
```

   a. schon\
   b. erst\
   c. noch\
   d. wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The shock is the point: eight has arrived sooner than you thought. *erst acht Uhr* would be the relaxed version — "only eight, plenty of time" — which is the wrong feeling for someone about to miss a train.

</details>

2. You invited guests for seven o'clock. At half past six the doorbell rings.\
   a. Sie sind noch da.\
   b. Sie sind wieder da.\
   c. Sie sind erst da.\
   d. Sie sind schon da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** They have arrived, and earlier than planned — that gap between fact and expectation is what *schon* marks. *noch da* would mean they never left.

</details>

3. Complete the dialogue. It is nine in the evening.

```text
A: Hast du ___ gegessen?
B: Ja, um sieben.
```

   a. noch\
   b. schon\
   c. erst\
   d. wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** In questions, *schon* asks "by now?" — English switches to "yet," German keeps *schon*. B's answer confirms it: the eating is already behind him.

</details>

4. **Wir warten schon eine Stunde.** What is *schon* doing to the hour?\
   a. It presents one hour as a small amount.\
   b. It says that it is exactly one hour.\
   c. It presents one hour as a large amount.\
   d. It says that the hour is just beginning.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** With an amount of time, *schon* colours the number as a lot. The opposite colouring comes from *erst*: *Wir warten erst eine Stunde* would mean "only an hour so far."

</details>

5. Your friend is nervous before an exam. You say: **Das schaffst du schon.**\
   a. You are asking about the result.\
   b. You are saying the exam is already over.\
   c. You are encouraging him.\
   d. You are saying the exam is too difficult.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** This is *schon* as reassurance — "you'll manage, don't worry." There is no "already" in it at all, which is why translating word by word goes wrong here.

</details>

6. In which sentence does *schon* **not** mean "already, earlier than expected"?\
   a. Er ist schon hier.\
   b. Das Restaurant ist schon zu.\
   c. Ich bin schon fertig.\
   d. Das wird schon.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Das wird schon* is the reassurance particle — "it'll be all right." Recognize it when you hear it; you don't need to build sentences with it yet.

</details>

## Part B — Tell It Apart

7. You have to leave the house at nine and your friend is still in the shower. Which sentence tells him to hurry?\
   a. Es ist erst acht Uhr.\
   b. Es ist schon acht Uhr.\
   c. Es ist noch nicht acht Uhr.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** Same clock, three different messages. *schon* = late already, get moving. *erst* = relax, there's time. *noch nicht* = it hasn't even reached eight.

</details>

8. What is the difference between **Ich lerne schon zwei Jahre Deutsch** and **Ich lerne erst zwei Jahre Deutsch**?\
   a. The first sentence presents two years as a lot; the second presents it as little.\
   b. The first sentence is past tense; the second is present tense.\
   c. The first sentence is a question.\
   d. A different person is learning in the first sentence.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** The facts are identical — two years either way. Only the speaker's attitude to that number changes. This pair is worth learning as a set.

</details>

9. You want to say: **I'm not finished yet.** A learner writes **Ich bin schon nicht fertig.** How would you judge it?\
   a. Wrong for this meaning — you need *noch nicht*.\
   b. Correct — exactly that meaning.\
   c. Correct, but very polite.\
   d. Correct, but only in questions.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** *schon nicht* is not the negative of *schon*. When you negate one of this pair, you reach for the other word: **Ich bin noch nicht fertig.**

</details>

10. Complete the dialogue.

```text
A: Warst du ___ mal in Deutschland?
B: Ja, letztes Jahr.
```

   a. noch\
   b. erst\
   c. wieder\
   d. schon

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *schon mal* asks about experience at any point up to now — "have you ever…?" The negative answer to the same question uses the other partner: *Nein, noch nie.*

</details>

11. **Schon wieder Regen!** What is the speaker feeling?\
   a. Happiness\
   b. Annoyance\
   c. Surprise without a positive or negative judgment\
   d. Fear

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** *schon wieder* almost always carries irritation — it says "again, and too soon." Plain *wieder Regen* would be closer to the neutral option c.

</details>

12. The film starts at eight. At quarter to eight, Tom walks into the cinema.\
   a. Tom ist noch da.\
   b. Tom ist schon da.\
   c. Tom ist wieder da.\
   d. Tom ist nicht mehr da.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** He has arrived ahead of the expected time. *noch da* would need him to have been there earlier and not left; *wieder da* would need him to have left and come back.

</details>

## Part C — Conversation and Judgement

13. Complete the dialogue. *(satt = full, having eaten enough)*

```text
A: Möchtest du noch Kartoffeln?
B: Nein danke, ich bin ___ satt.
```

   a. schon\
   b. erst\
   c. noch\
   d. wieder

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a**

**Explanation:** B has reached "full" sooner than the offer assumed — a polite way of declining. Notice how A's *noch* and B's *schon* work as a natural pair: one offers more, the other says the point has already been reached.

</details>

14. A learner wants to ask "Isn't the train here yet?" and writes **Ist der Zug schon nicht da?** What should it be?\
   a. Ist der Zug nicht schon da?\
   b. Ist der Zug noch nicht da?\
   c. Ist der Zug wieder nicht da?\
   d. Ist der Zug erst nicht da?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **b**

**Explanation:** "Not yet" is *noch nicht*, in that fixed order. Option a is real German, but it means something else — roughly "isn't it here already?", expecting that it is.

</details>

15. You are meeting a friend at a restaurant at 19:15. At 19:00 you text: **Bist du schon da?** What are you asking?\
   a. Whether he is about to leave.\
   b. Whether he knows the way.\
   c. Whether he has already arrived by that time.\
   d. Whether he is still at the restaurant.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** *schon* in a question asks about "by now" — has the arrival happened ahead of the agreed time? Option d would be *Bist du noch da?*, which assumes he was there and asks whether that is still true.

</details>

16. You run into your flatmate's brother for the third time in one day.\
   a. Wieder du!\
   b. Noch du!\
   c. Erst du!\
   d. Schon wieder du!

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** *Schon wieder du!* is the everyday exclamation — "you again!" — with the mild, usually joking irritation built in. Bare *Wieder du* is understandable but not what people say.

</details>

17. Compare **Er ist hier** and **Er ist schon hier.** What does *schon* add?\
   a. The second sentence is a question.\
   b. The second sentence is more polite.\
   c. The second sentence says he is here earlier than expected.\
   d. The second sentence says he is staying for a long time.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **c**

**Explanation:** The fact — he is here — is identical in both. *schon* adds a relationship between that fact and what someone expected, which is what these small words do generally.

</details>

18. You walk up to the bakery at 17:00 and find it shut. It normally closes at 18:00.\
   a. Der Laden ist noch zu.\
   b. Der Laden ist wieder zu.\
   c. Der Laden ist erst zu.\
   d. Der Laden ist schon zu.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **d**

**Explanation:** Closed, and earlier than it should be. *noch zu* would mean it hasn't opened yet today; *wieder zu* would mean it opened, shut, and you're commenting on the repeat.

</details>

## Part D — Retrieve and Produce

19. **Select all that apply.** In which sentences is *schon* working as reassurance rather than "already"?\
   a. Das wird schon.\
   b. Er ist schon hier.\
   c. Er kommt schon.\
   d. Das schaffst du schon.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **a, c, d**

**Explanation:** All three mean roughly "don't worry." Only b is the ordinary "already" use. Note that c is genuinely ambiguous out of context — with heavy stress it can be impatient — but in a reassuring conversation it means "he'll come, relax."

</details>

20. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   You have been standing on the platform since two o'clock. It is now three, and the train still hasn't come. You turn to your friend.

```text
Wir warten ___ eine Stunde!
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **schon**

**Explanation:** *Wir warten schon eine Stunde!* — an hour, and that is a lot. Swapping in *erst* would turn the complaint into patience: "it's only been an hour."

</details>

21. Fill in the missing word. No options given.

   A colleague sent you an email five minutes ago and asks whether you have seen it. You have.

```text
Ja, ich habe sie ___ gelesen.
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **schon**

**Explanation:** Done, and faster than she expected — exactly the *schon* situation. (*die E-Mail* is feminine, hence *sie*.)

</details>

22. Put the words in order to ask a guest whether they have eaten.

```text
du / gegessen / schon / hast
```

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Hast du schon gegessen?**

**Explanation:** Verb first in a yes/no question, past participle at the end, and *schon* in the middle. This is one of the most useful ready-made sentences on the page.

</details>

23. Rewrite the sentence for the new situation.

   Original: **Es ist erst neun Uhr.**\
   New situation: your meeting started at nine, you are still on the bus, and you are stressed.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Es ist schon neun Uhr.**

**Explanation:** The clock doesn't change — your relationship to it does. *erst* says "plenty of time," *schon* says "too late already."

</details>

24. **Produce it:** Someone asks whether you have ever been to Germany. Say that you have.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** **Ja, ich war schon mal in Deutschland.**

**Explanation:** *schon mal* covers "at some point before now." A shorter *Ja, schon mal* works in conversation, and if the answer were no, the natural German is *Nein, noch nie* rather than a negated *schon*.

</details>
